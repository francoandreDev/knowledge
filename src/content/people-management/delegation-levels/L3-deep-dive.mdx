## The "before" conversation, in full

> **Manager:** "Hey, can you take care of picking a new CI vendor for the team? Our
> current one's contract is up next month."
>
> **Report:** "Sure thing, I'll look into it."

That's the entire delegation. No level was named — "take care of it" was left to carry the
full weight of defining scope, authority, and check-in expectations, and it wasn't built
to carry that weight. Two weeks later:

> **Report:** "Good news — I found a great CI vendor, way better pricing than what we
> have. Signed a one-year contract this morning, so we're all set for next month."
>
> **Manager:** "...Signed already? I hadn't seen any options yet — did you look at
> [Vendor X]? We used them on the platform team and they were great for our exact setup."
>
> **Report:** "I didn't know about that one. I can look into switching, but we're now
> locked into this contract for a year unless we eat the cancellation fee."

Notice what actually broke: not the report's research (which may have been genuinely
solid), and not their judgment about pricing — the process itself skipped the one step
(a shortlist review before commitment) that the manager's mental model assumed was
built in.

## The "after" conversation — one added sentence

> **Manager:** "Hey, can you take care of picking a new CI vendor for the team? Our
> current one's contract is up next month. Go ahead and research options — loop me in
> once you've got it down to two or three finalists, before signing anything. I want to
> weigh in on the final call, but I don't need to be involved in the research itself."
>
> **Report:** "Got it — I'll do the research and bring you a shortlist before we commit
> to anything."

Two weeks later:

> **Report:** "Narrowed it down to three finalists — here's a comparison. I'm leaning
> toward [Vendor Y] for the pricing, but wanted your take before moving forward."
>
> **Manager:** "This is great work. One thing — we used [Vendor X] on the platform team
> and they had a much smoother setup for our exact stack. Worth a look before we decide?"
>
> **Report:** "I hadn't considered them — I'll add them to the comparison and circle back
> before signing anything."

The research work is identical in both versions — the report did real, careful evaluation
either way. **The only structural difference is one added sentence naming the level (Level
3: research and recommend) and one specific trigger for the check-in (finalists, before
signing).** That single sentence is what let the manager's context (knowledge of Vendor X)
actually reach the decision before it became irreversible, instead of after.

## Extend the scenario: what if the stakes were even higher?

This incident's fix — one sentence naming the level and the check-in trigger — is one
case, not the whole territory. **What changes if, instead of a CI vendor, the delegated
decision is hiring a contractor for a six-month engagement at a much larger cost, with a
much harder-to-reverse commitment?** Trace it through: the same two-question framework
from L2 still applies (reversibility, and the delegate's existing context) — but a
six-month contractor hire is high-stakes enough that the appropriate level likely shifts
down the spectrum, from "research and recommend" (Level 3) to something closer to "narrow
to two finalists and I'll personally interview them before we decide" (a more
tightly-scoped Level 2-3 hybrid), reflecting that the cost of a wrong call is
proportionally larger. The mechanism — naming the level and a specific check-in trigger
out loud — doesn't change; what changes is _where on the spectrum_ that level should sit,
based on the same two questions applied to higher stakes.

**A second question worth sitting with:** what if the report, instead of a first-timer,
had successfully run this exact kind of vendor evaluation three times before? The
reversibility of the decision hasn't changed — but the delegate's demonstrated context
has, which per L2 is real grounds to shift the level up (toward Level 4 or 5) rather than
repeating the same tightly-scoped Level 3 by default, regardless of track record.

## Failure modes

| Failure mode                                                             | What it gets wrong                                                                                                                                                               |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Assuming "take care of it" communicates a specific level                 | Vague delegation verbs get filled in by whichever assumption is more natural to each person — which is exactly how the two mental models diverged                                |
| Treating a delegation mismatch as a competence problem                   | The report's research may have been excellent — the incident was a process gap (no check-in point), not a skill gap                                                              |
| Naming a level once and assuming it applies to every future task         | The right level depends on THIS decision's stakes and THIS person's context for it — a Level 5 on one task doesn't imply Level 5 on a different, higher-stakes one               |
| Treating "trust but verify" as checking in on every step                 | Effective verification is one specific, well-chosen trigger point (like "before signing anything"), not constant oversight — the latter is just micromanagement with extra steps |
| Reacting to one mismatch by permanently lowering the delegate's autonomy | A single incident calls for a clearer level-setting conversation next time, not a blanket demotion to Level 1 on everything going forward                                        |
