## The "before" conversation

> **Manager:** "Can you take care of picking a new CI vendor for the team? Our current
> contract is up next month."
>
> **Report:** "Sure, I'll look into it."

No level was named. "Take care of it" had to carry scope, authority, and check-in
expectations, and it was never built for that. Two weeks later:

> **Report:** "Good news: better pricing than what we have, and I signed a one-year
> contract this morning."
>
> **Manager:** "Signed already? I hadn't seen any options. Did you look at Vendor A? The
> platform team used it and it fit our setup well."
>
> **Report:** "I didn't know about that one. We are locked in now unless we pay the
> cancellation fee."

## The "after" conversation: one added sentence

> **Manager:** "Can you take care of picking a new CI vendor? Our current contract is up
> next month. Research options and loop me in once you have two or three finalists, before
> signing anything. I want to weigh in on the final call, but I don't need to be involved
> in the research itself."
>
> **Report:** "Got it. I'll bring you a shortlist before we commit to anything."

Two weeks later the report brings three finalists and a lean toward Vendor B. The manager
mentions Vendor A from the platform team; the report adds it to the comparison and circles
back before signing.

```mermaid
sequenceDiagram
    participant M as Manager

    R->>M: Three finalists and a lean
    M->>R: Add Vendor A to the comparison
    R->>M: Added; will confirm before signing
```

## Was this a skill problem or a process problem?

The research work is identical in both versions. The only structural difference is one
sentence naming the level (Level 3) and one trigger (finalists, before signing). That
sentence gave the manager's knowledge of Vendor A one moment to reach the decision while it
was still reversible.

<Checkpoint question="In the 'after' version the report did the same research and the manager was just as absent day to day. What exactly changed the outcome?">

The work did not change. What changed was that the level and one check-in trigger were
named, which created a single scheduled moment where the manager's relevant knowledge could
enter the decision before it became irreversible. Without that named moment, the knowledge
had no way to arrive in time, no matter how good the research was.

## What if the stakes, or the report's track record, were different?

This fix, one sentence naming the level and the check-in, is one case, not the whole
territory. **Suppose the delegated decision were hiring a contractor for a six-month
engagement, a larger and harder-to-reverse commitment. Where on the spectrum should the
level sit, and what would the check-in trigger be?** Trace it with the two questions from
L2. Reversibility is worse, so the level likely moves down toward Level 2 or 3, perhaps
with the manager joining the final interview. The mechanism stays the same; only the
position on the spectrum changes.

**A second question to extend it:** what if the report had already run this exact kind of
vendor evaluation three times successfully? Reversibility is unchanged, but demonstrated
context has grown, which is real grounds to move toward Level 4 on the next one.

| Situation                                        | Likely level | Check-in trigger                    |
| ------------------------------------------------ | ------------ | ----------------------------------- |
| First vendor evaluation, year-long contract      | 3            | Finalists, before signing           |
| Third similar evaluation, same stakes            | 4            | Tell me the decision before signing |
| Draft of an internal announcement                | 5            | Tell me after it is sent            |
| Six-month contractor hire, first time delegating | 2 to 3       | Manager joins the final interview   |

<Checkpoint question="A report nails a Level 3 delegation with a strong recommendation. Does that earn them a higher level on every future task?">

No. The level is an assessment of demonstrated context for _this kind of decision_, not a
rating of the person overall. A strong vendor recommendation is grounds for more autonomy on
similar vendor decisions. A different, higher-stakes decision gets its own read of
reversibility and context.

## Failure modes

| Failure mode                                                     | What it gets wrong                                                                                   |
| ---------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Assuming "take care of it" communicates a level                  | Each person fills the gap with their own assumption, which is how the two mental models diverged     |
| Treating a mismatch as a competence problem                      | The research may have been excellent; the missing piece was a check-in point                         |
| Naming a level once and assuming it applies to every future task | The right level depends on this decision's stakes and this person's context for it                   |
| Treating "trust but verify" as checking every step               | Good verification is one well-chosen trigger; constant oversight is micromanagement with extra steps |
| Permanently lowering autonomy after one mismatch                 | One incident calls for a clearer conversation next time, not a blanket drop to Level 1               |
