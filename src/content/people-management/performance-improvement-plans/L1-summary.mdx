A manager has been quietly worried about a report's performance for two full
quarters: missed deadlines, work that needs heavy rework, a pattern that is not
improving. The manager mentioned it once, gently, in a 1:1, and otherwise kept
hoping it would resolve on its own. Now the manager is ready to act, but when
they raise it directly, the report is genuinely shocked: **"Nobody ever told me
this was serious enough to put my job at risk."** The manager was not lying
to themselves about the performance. So why does the report have a point?

</Scenario>

The manager's worry was real and the report's surprise is also real. Both can
be true at once, because what the manager _meant_ and what the report _heard_
were two different messages. The rest of this unit is about closing that gap on
purpose.

## What is a PIP actually for?

**If the manager already knows the performance is poor, why not simply say
"you are failing" and decide at the end of the quarter?** Because "failing" with
no definition leaves both people arguing about a feeling. A **performance
improvement plan (PIP)** replaces the feeling with a written, time-bound
statement of what acceptable looks like, so "did they improve" has a yes/no
answer.

| Without a plan                                  | With a plan                                             |
| ----------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| "Try to be more consistent about deadlines"     | "Ship the June 15 release on or before its stated date" |
| No end date, so the worry just keeps running    | A defined period with dated checkpoints                 |
| Manager's impression versus report's impression | A fact both can check                                   |

## The no-surprises test

**By the time a PIP starts, what should the report already know?** That their
performance is a serious problem. The **no-surprises principle** says the PIP
formalizes something already communicated; it must not be the first time the
report hears it. The Scenario fails this test: one gentle mention, never framed
as a warning, reads as ordinary coaching.

- The test is about what the report _understood_, not what the manager _said_.
- A PIP that opens with news the report has never heard looks sudden, and
  sudden looks unfair even when the underlying problem is real.
- Passing the test is what makes the plan feel like help with a known problem
  rather than a verdict.

## Key terms

- **Performance improvement plan (PIP)** — a formal, written, time-bound
  document stating what performance must look like, by when, to be considered
  acceptable. Used when informal coaching has not closed a real gap.
- **No-surprises principle** — nothing in a PIP should be the report's first
  time hearing that their performance is a serious problem.
- **Documentation trail** — the ongoing, dated record of specific performance
  conversations and examples, kept _before_ a PIP starts, so the PIP is
  defensible rather than sudden or retaliatory.
- **Specific, observable expectation** — a goal stated so concretely that
  whether it was met is a factual question, not an opinion.

## What this unit covers

L2 works through the sequence a fair process follows, from the first informal
signal to the final checkpoint, and why jumping straight to a PIP feels sudden
even when the problem was real. L3 runs the Scenario's manager and report
through a full PIP: the repair conversation, the written plan, and both
outcomes at the final checkpoint.

_Rules about notice periods, probation, required process, and what the
employer must document differ by country, region, and company. This unit
teaches the management mechanics; check your HR partner and local rules before
acting._
