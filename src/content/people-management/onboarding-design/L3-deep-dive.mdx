## Why must the first task be real?

**A manager builds a polished sandbox exercise: a fake bug in a throwaway
repo, safe and easy to check. Does it satisfy "small, real, scoped"?** It is
small and scoped but not real, and "real" is the ingredient doing the work.

| First task         | Teaches the tools | Teaches the real system and norms | Trust signal both ways |
| ------------------ | ----------------- | --------------------------------- | ---------------------- |
| Sandbox exercise   | Yes               | No                                | Weak                   |
| Small real bug fix | Yes               | Yes, as a side effect             | Yes                    |

A real task pulls the new hire through the actual code, the actual review
process and the team's real habits, none of which a toy can imitate. Shipping
something small and real also lets the team see how the person works, early and
at low stakes. Day 1 for Tomas becomes: a working local setup with a trivial
change (a typo fix) by the end of the day, one well-defined bug with a testable
"done", and a named buddy.

<Checkpoint question="Setup is done by lunch on day 1, and the real task is a 2-day bug fix. Why still make the typo fix part of the plan?">

The typo fix is not the task; it is a check that the tools work before anything
harder depends on them. If the environment is broken, finding out through a typo
costs minutes, while finding it out in the middle of the real task costs a
confusing day in which the new hire cannot tell a tooling fault from their own
mistake.

</Checkpoint>

## Does the same plan fit an experienced hire?

**The next hire has ten years of engineering behind them and none here. Do they
need the ramp plan?** The mechanism still holds: unanchored information does not
stick whoever receives it. What shifts is where the support goes.

| Missing piece        | New engineer                               | Experienced hire from elsewhere                                          |
| -------------------- | ------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Technical execution  | Often needs hand-holding on the first task | Usually needs little                                                     |
| Org context          | Often not yet noticed as a gap             | The main gap: who decides what, which parts are fragile, whom to loop in |
| Where the plan leans | Scoped task plus technical pairing         | Scoped task plus decision history and owners                             |

If an experienced hire says "I do not need a ramp plan" and gets unstructured
full access on day 1, the Tomas mistake returns in a new costume. They may move
fast technically, which makes early missteps look like confidence rather than a
missing map.

<Checkpoint question="An experienced hire is quick on their first task but ships a change to a fragile area without telling its owners. Is this a skill problem or a plan problem?">

A plan problem. Their skill is not in doubt; what they lacked was org context:
which areas are fragile and whom to tell. A ramp plan for them would have named
that early, through the buddy or a short list of owners. Treating it as a
competence issue would misdiagnose the gap and teach the wrong lesson.

</Checkpoint>

## What do the 30, 60 and 90 day checkpoints actually do?

**If the plan is written on day 1, why check it three more times?** Because a
plan is a hypothesis about how fast one particular person will ramp.

| Checkpoint | Compare                                          | Possible adjustment                       |
| ---------- | ------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------- |
| 30 days    | Tasks shipped vs. the plan's expectation         | Resize upcoming tasks up or down          |
| 60 days    | Autonomy vs. how much buddy help is still needed | Taper the buddy, or extend the support    |
| 90 days    | Ownership of an area vs. the plan                | Set the next goals, since ramp-up is over |

A fixed script that ignores how the person is actually doing cannot catch a gap
that is opening up. A checkpoint turns "we assumed" into "we looked".

## What this worked example covers, and what it does not

Tomas's redesign is a case, not the whole territory. The core idea (a small,
real, scoped task early, with context pulled in by the work) carries over to any
new tool, team or role. The specifics (an environment setup, a bug fix,
30/60/90 marks) fit a software engineer.

**Try extending it yourself.** If the new hire joined a support or operations
role, where there is no single well-defined bug to fix, what would "a small,
real, scoped first task" look like, and would the buddy's role change? And
what if the news were worse: the buddy is pulled onto an incident in week 1.
Which part of the plan breaks first?

## Failure modes

| Failure mode                                    | What it gets wrong                                                     |
| ----------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Treating better documents as the fix            | Quality is not the issue if none of it is tied to current work         |
| No starting task, called autonomy               | Without a scoped task nothing tells the new hire what to explore first |
| Assuming experience removes the need for a plan | Experience supplies skill, not this team's decisions and owners        |
| Never revisiting the plan                       | Without checkpoints, a gap can grow unseen for weeks                   |
