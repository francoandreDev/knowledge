## What would each of them say, and what would they mean?

**Ask both "what's wrong" and you hear a personality clash. Ask what it would
mean if their complaint were true and you hear something else. What?**

| Person | Position (what they say)                                        | Likely interest underneath                                       |
| ------ | --------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Priya  | "Devon nitpicks every PR with unnecessary comments."            | Wants her design judgment trusted, not read as a challenge       |
| Devon  | "Priya pushes back instead of just addressing review feedback." | Wants his careful review engaged with, not waved off in one line |

The positions sound like opposites: "nitpicky" against "defensive." The
interests are near mirror images: **each feels the other does not respect
their competence.** That symmetry is invisible at the position level and
appears only once each interest is surfaced separately.

<Checkpoint question="A teammate says a coworker 'always talks over me in meetings.' That is a position. What question starts to find the interest, and how is asking it different from agreeing the coworker is rude?">

Ask something like "what does it feel like when that happens?" It differs from
agreeing because it does not accept the complaint as the finished story. It
treats "talks over me" as the visible layer and goes looking underneath, the
way Priya's "he nitpicks" became "he doesn't think I know what I'm doing."
Agreeing instead would pick a side before the other person is heard at all and
make the real interest harder to reach later.

</Checkpoint>

## Why does neutrality take discipline?

**Sam may already feel one of them is more in the wrong. Why does it matter
if that leaks out?** Because once a mediator signals agreement with one side,
the other stops treating the process as a search for the real issue and starts
treating it as two against one.

Neutrality does not mean pretending both are right about every claim. It means
the mediator's role is **structural** (making room for both interests to
surface), not **judicial** (deciding whose position wins). Two behaviors test
it most directly:

1. **Do not propose the solution first.** A mediator who announces "here is what
   you both should do" has replaced the two people's negotiation with their own.
   Even a good solution imposed this way tends not to stick, because neither
   person did the work of understanding the other.
2. **Do not repeat one side's framing as settled fact.** "Priya, I hear you're
   frustrated with Devon's nitpicking," said in front of Devon, adopts Priya's
   word "nitpicking" as the agreed description. Devon will reject it before the
   real conversation starts.

<Checkpoint question="Before talking to Devon at all, Sam tells Priya 'honestly, I think you're right about this.' What does that do to the mediation?">

It ends the mediation's neutrality before it begins. Priya now has the
validation she wanted, so she has less reason to explore her own interest
honestly, and Devon, who usually finds out, will read every later step as
biased. The harm is not that the opinion might be wrong; it is that voicing it
turns Sam from someone structuring a conversation into a judge who has already
ruled.

</Checkpoint>

Neutral is also **not passive**. A neutral mediator is active: structuring the
conversations, asking questions that surface interests, and keeping people
from staying stuck at the position level.

| Mistake                                              | Why it fails                                                                                     |
| ---------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Concluding the clash is an unfixable personality gap | A recurring clash after a surface fix usually means the wrong layer was addressed                |
| Treating neutral as staying out of the way           | Nobody structures the conversation, so it stays at the position level                            |
| Assuming every conflict is a mirror image            | Sometimes one side's interest is plainly reasonable and the other's position is the core problem |
