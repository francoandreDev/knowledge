Priya and Marcus both report into a team that just got a new
director. Within a month, the director starts routing Priya and
Marcus's joint project through a third engineer's name in every
leadership update — same unfair move, aimed at both of them equally.
Marcus has plenty of friendly colleagues, people he gets lunch with,
people who'd agree in private that the reorg looks off. None of them
say anything to the director. Priya has spent two years being
genuinely, visibly useful to a senior engineer on another team —
reviewing his gnarliest PRs carefully, flagging a production risk
before it shipped, showing up prepared every time he needed a second
opinion. When he hears about the reorg secondhand, he brings it up
with the director himself, unprompted, in a meeting Priya isn't even
in: **"I've worked closely with her for two years. That doesn't match
what I've seen her actually drive."** Priya didn't ask him to do
that. He did it because two years of being useful had already told
him she was worth vouching for. **Marcus has no one who can make that
call, because no one has ever had a reason to.**

## The shape of the problem

- [white](/corporate-politics/white/) covers legitimate influence in
  general — reciprocity, alliance-building, being visibly useful as
  the way real influence gets built. **This unit is a specific
  application of that idea: building alliances _for defensive
  purposes_, in advance of needing them**, not influence-building as
  a general career skill.
- Most people have mentors. Far fewer have sponsors, and most people
  who only have mentors mistakenly believe that's the same
  protection — it isn't. A mentor's advice never leaves the
  conversation you had it in. A sponsor's advocacy happens precisely
  in the rooms where you have no voice at all.
- The single biggest mistake is trying to build this kind of alliance
  _after_ trouble has already started. Real trust and a real track
  record take time to build — they can't be manufactured urgently in
  a crisis, and a sudden burst of attentiveness aimed at someone with
  influence, right when you need help, reads as exactly what it is.

## Key terms

- **Mentor** — someone who gives you advice, privately, when you ask
  for it. Valuable, but their support never has to leave the room you
  had the conversation in.
- **Sponsor** — someone who uses their own real influence and
  credibility to advocate for you when you're not present — actively
  vouching for you, putting part of their own reputation behind
  yours. This is the protection a mentor's advice can't provide.
- **Coalition-building as defense** — deliberately investing in
  genuine, useful relationships with people who have real standing,
  before you need their protection, so that if an unfair situation
  ever arises, someone with influence is both willing and positioned
  to act on your behalf.
- **The trust-and-track-record timeline** — the honest fact that
  sponsorship is earned through a sustained pattern of being useful
  and reliable, not through a single favor or a well-timed
  compliment; it connects directly to
  [favor-economy](/corporate-politics/favor-economy/)'s idea of
  banking goodwill long before you ever need to spend it.
- **The honesty line** — the difference between genuinely being
  useful and trustworthy to someone versus performing friendliness
  purely as a self-protective transaction; a coalition built on the
  second version tends to be fragile and collapses exactly when you
  need it most, because it was never real.

## What this unit covers

L2 builds the sponsor vs. mentor model in full, with a worked
contrast of what each one actually does in a moment of real stakes,
and lays out why this kind of coalition-building has to start well
before trouble shows up. L3 follows Priya through the real,
unglamorous groundwork of building her sponsor relationship over two
years — and the moment it pays off.
