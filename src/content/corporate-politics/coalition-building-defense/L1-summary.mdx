A new director takes over and, within a month, starts routing Priya and
Marcus's joint project through a third engineer's name in every leadership
update. Same unfair move, aimed at both of them equally.

Marcus has plenty of friendly colleagues. They would agree in private that the
reorg looks off, and none of them say anything to the director. Priya spent two
years being genuinely useful to a senior engineer on another team: careful
reviews of his hardest PRs, a production risk flagged before it shipped,
always prepared when he needed a second opinion. When he hears about the reorg
secondhand, he raises it with the director himself, in a meeting Priya is not
in: **"I've worked closely with her for two years. That doesn't match what
I've seen her actually drive."** She never asked him to.

**Marcus has no one who can make that call, because no one ever had a reason
to. What is the difference between Priya's two years and Marcus's friendly
colleagues?**

## The shape of the problem

Marcus's friends and Priya's senior colleague both "like" them. Only one of
those relationships can act when the person is absent.

| Question                          | Mentor                       | Sponsor                                     |
| --------------------------------- | ---------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| What do they give you?            | Advice, when you ask         | Advocacy, often unprompted                  |
| Where does it happen?             | In the conversation with you | In rooms you are not in                     |
| What does it cost them?           | Their time                   | Part of their own reputation                |
| What does it protect you against? | Your own bad decisions       | Decisions made about you without your voice |

Most people have mentors. Far fewer have sponsors, and people holding only
mentors often believe they have the same protection. They do not: a mentor's
advice never leaves the conversation it was given in.

[white](/corporate-politics/white/) covers legitimate influence in general.
This unit is one specific use of it: building genuine alliances _for defensive
purposes_, in advance, so that someone with standing is both willing and
positioned to act if an unfair situation ever appears.

## Key terms

- **Mentor**: advises privately when asked. Valuable, but the support stays in
  the room where it was given.
- **Sponsor**: uses their own credibility to advocate for you when you are not
  present, putting part of their reputation behind yours.
- **Coalition-building as defense**: investing in genuine, useful
  relationships with people who have standing, before you need protection.
- **Track record**: a sustained pattern of useful, reliable work. Sponsorship
  is earned through it, not through one favor or a well-timed compliment; see
  [favor-economy](/corporate-politics/favor-economy/) for banking goodwill
  early.
- **The honesty line**: the difference between being useful to someone and
  performing friendliness only because they might protect you someday.

## What the other levels cover

L2 builds the model: what a sponsor does that a mentor cannot, how long the
track record takes, and where honest alliance-building ends and manipulation
begins. L3 follows Priya through the two years of groundwork, a moment where
she has to check her own motives, and the room she was not in.
