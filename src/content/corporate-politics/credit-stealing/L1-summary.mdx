proposed pricing change. In the executive readout, a colleague
presents the analysis as their own work — "here's the approach I

impressed. Your colleague gets visible credit for three weeks of
your work, in front of the people whose opinion of you matters most
for your next promotion cycle. **This wasn't a misunderstanding about
who did what — your colleague knew exactly whose analysis this was.
So what actually happened here, and does anything useful come from
correctly naming it?**

## What separates black politics from ordinary influence?

**Two colleagues both come out of the same readout looking good. One presented a
team's model and said "my teammate built this; here is what it shows." The other
said "the approach I came up with" about work someone else built. Both gained
visibility, so what is different?**

| Question                                        | Names the builder openly | Claims the builder's work |
| ----------------------------------------------- | ------------------------ | ------------------------- |
| Could the presenter say what they did out loud? | Yes, nothing is hidden   | No, it only works unseen  |
| Whose standing goes up?                         | Both people              | Only the presenter        |
| Does it need a blurred fact to work?            | No                       | Yes, who built it         |

The unit `white` in this track covered legitimate influence-building: tactics that
advance shared goals transparently. **Black politics** is the counterpart: tactics
that advance one person's position at someone else's expense, usually through
deception, concealment, or deniability rather than open persuasion. The dividing line
is not "trying to influence people", which every workplace involves. It is whether the
move could survive being explained openly.

This unit is about **recognizing** these moves and responding well when one lands on
you. It describes what they look like so you can spot them; it is not a how-to for
doing them.

## Key terms

- **Credit-stealing** — presenting someone else's work, analysis, or idea as your own,
  or allowing others to believe it's yours without correcting them.
- **Scapegoating** — deliberately directing blame for a shared or someone else's
  failure onto a person who wasn't actually responsible for it.
- **Sabotage** — deliberately weakening someone else's work or reputation, often while
  appearing helpful or neutral.
- **Weaponized rumors** — spreading unverified or false information as a reputation
  weapon, while keeping the source hard to trace.
- **Manufactured urgency** — falsely framing a request or decision as more time-critical
  than it actually is, typically to bypass scrutiny.
- **Deniability** — leaving yourself an innocent-sounding excuse after the damage is
  done: "I forgot," "I thought everyone knew," or "I did not mean it that way."

## Why does reading it accurately matter?

**If a colleague's name was left off a slide, is the right reaction outrage or a shrug?**
Neither, always. Two kinds of mistake are possible, and each has a cost:

| What was really happening   | You read it as an attack                          | You read it as an accident             |
| --------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------- |
| A genuine oversight         | Trust burned for nothing                          | Correct, nothing more needed           |
| A deliberate, repeated move | Correct, the response can match what is happening | Left unaddressed, and likely to repeat |

In plain language: the issue is who appears to have done the work, who gets thanked for
it, and whose reputation grows from it. **Intent** means whether the person meant to do it
or it was a mistake. **Deniability** means they can say "I did not mean to leave you out"
even though the room already credited them. The Scenario is written as a fairly clear
case, but real life often starts more ambiguous, so the response needs calibration.

## What this unit covers

L2 maps the five black-politics tactics (credit-stealing, scapegoating, sabotage,
weaponized rumors, manufactured urgency) against what white politics looks like, and
covers why deniability is the common thread that makes all of them hard to call out. L3
goes deep on the Scenario's credit-stealing case: how to tell a genuine pattern from an
ambiguous one, and what a calibrated, evidence-based response looks like in the moment
and afterward.

Credit-stealing is the main worked case. The other tactics sit beside it so you can see
the broader contrast with [white politics](/corporate-politics/white/): influence that
can survive being explained openly versus influence that depends on hiding the real move.
