You spend three weeks building the cost-modeling analysis behind a
proposed pricing change. In the executive readout, a colleague
presents the analysis as their own work — "here's the approach I
came up with" — without mentioning your name. Leadership is
impressed. Your colleague gets visible credit for three weeks of
your work, in front of the people whose opinion of you matters most
for your next promotion cycle. **This wasn't a misunderstanding about
who did what — your colleague knew exactly whose analysis this was.
So what actually happened here, and does anything useful come from
correctly naming it?**

## The shape of the problem

- The unit `white` in this track covered legitimate influence-building
  — tactics that advance shared goals transparently. **"Black"
  politics** is the counterpart: tactics that advance one person's
  position at someone else's expense, usually through deception,
  concealment, or deniability rather than open persuasion.
- **Credit-stealing** — presenting someone else's work or ideas as
  your own — is one of the most common black-politics tactics because
  it's low-risk for the person doing it: it's often deniable
  ("I didn't mean to leave you out") and hard to prove intent behind.
- Recognizing black politics accurately matters because the response
  to a genuine misunderstanding is different from the response to a
  deliberate, repeated pattern — treating every ambiguous case as an
  attack burns trust and credibility, but treating a real pattern as
  an innocent mistake leaves it unaddressed and likely to repeat.

In plain language: the issue is who appears to have done the work, who
gets thanked for it, and whose reputation grows from it. **Intent**
means whether the person meant to do it or it was a mistake.
**Deniability** means they can say "I did not mean to leave you out"
even though the room already credited them. This scenario is written as
a fairly clear case, but real life often starts more ambiguous, so the
response still needs calibration.

## Key terms

- **Black politics** — political tactics that advance someone's
  position at another person's expense, typically through
  concealment, deception, or deniability rather than transparent
  persuasion.
- **Credit-stealing** — presenting someone else's work, analysis, or
  idea as your own, or allowing others to believe it's yours without
  correcting them.
- **Scapegoating** — deliberately directing blame for a shared or
  someone else's failure onto a person who wasn't actually
  responsible for it.
- **Sabotage** — deliberately weakening someone else's work or
  reputation, often while appearing helpful or neutral.
- **Weaponized rumors** — spreading unverified or false information as
  a reputation weapon, while keeping the source hard to trace.
- **Manufactured urgency** — falsely framing a request or decision as
  more time-critical than it actually is, typically to bypass normal
  scrutiny or push a decision through before objections can form.
- **Deniability** — leaving yourself an innocent-sounding excuse after
  the damage is done: "I forgot," "I thought everyone knew," or "I did
  not mean it that way."

## What this unit covers

L2 maps the five black-politics tactics named in this unit's problem
statement (credit-stealing, scapegoating, sabotage, weaponized
rumors, manufactured urgency) against what "white" politics looks
like for contrast, and covers why deniability is the common thread
that makes all of them hard to call out directly. L3 goes deep on the
Scenario's credit-stealing case specifically — how to tell a genuine
pattern from an ambiguous one, and what a calibrated, evidence-based
response looks like in the moment and afterward.

This unit uses credit-stealing as the main worked case. The other
tactics are included so you can place it inside the broader contrast
with [white politics](/corporate-politics/white/): influence that can
survive being explained openly versus influence that depends on hiding
the real move.
