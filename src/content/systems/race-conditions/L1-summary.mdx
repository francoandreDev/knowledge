A flash-sale item has exactly 1 unit left. Two buyers click "buy" a few
milliseconds apart. Each request checks the stock, sees 1, and
proceeds to charge the card and decrement the count. Both requests get
a "purchase successful" response. The warehouse only has one unit to
ship. **The code that checked "is stock greater than zero" before
decrementing was correct, on its own. What actually went wrong?**

</Scenario>

## The shape of the problem

- Two requests can each read the same shared value, both see it as
  "available," and both act on that reading before either one's update
  is visible to the other — the check was true when read, and false by
  the time it mattered.
- This isn't a bug in either request's logic in isolation — reading
  "stock is 1" and then decrementing it is correct code. The bug is in
  the _gap_ between reading and acting, which another request can slip
  into.
- Fixing this doesn't mean writing more careful application code — it
  means making the read-then-act sequence itself impossible to
  interrupt, or serializing access to the shared value.

## Key terms

- **Race condition** — a bug where the correctness of a result depends
  on the relative timing of two or more operations that touch shared
  state, rather than on the logic of either operation alone.
- **Critical section** — the specific span of code, from reading a
  shared value to finishing the update based on it, that must not be
  interrupted by another operation touching the same value.
- **Atomicity** — a guarantee that an operation (or a sequence of them)
  happens as a single, indivisible step from every other operation's
  point of view — nothing else can observe it half-done.
- **Lock (mutex)** — a mechanism that lets only one operation enter a
  critical section at a time, forcing others to wait their turn instead
  of interleaving.

## What this unit covers

L2 works through exactly where the gap opens up — the "check, then
act" pattern known as time-of-check to time-of-use — and the two ways
to close it: making the operation atomic, or locking the critical
section. L3 reproduces the oversold-item bug with real, runnable code,
measures it actually failing, and fixes it two different ways.
