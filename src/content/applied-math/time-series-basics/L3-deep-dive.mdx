## Where do slope and intercept actually come from?

**Computing a fit is the one place where the arithmetic is the idea. What does
it take?** Three steps on the signups: find the average week (6.5) and average
signups (49.67); for each week multiply how far it is from the average week by
how far its signups are from the average signups, and add those up (792); add up
the squared distance of each week from the average week (143). The slope is the
first sum over the second.

```js
function linearRegression(xs, ys) {
  const n = xs.length;
  const xMean = xs.reduce((a, b) => a + b, 0) / n;
  const yMean = ys.reduce((a, b) => a + b, 0) / n;
  let num = 0;
  let den = 0;
  for (let i = 0; i < n; i++) {
    num += (xs[i] - xMean) * (ys[i] - yMean);
    den += (xs[i] - xMean) ** 2;
  }
  const slope = num / den;
  return { slope, intercept: yMean - slope * xMean };
}
```

For all twelve weeks the slope is 792 / 143 = 5.54 and the intercept is
49.67 - 5.54 x 6.5 = 13.67. For weeks 1-10 the sums are 168 and 82.5, so the
slope is 2.04 and the intercept 28.40. Adding two viral weeks multiplied the
slope by 5.54 / 2.04 = 2.7.

<Checkpoint question="The 12-week slope is about 5.54. Does that mean week 12 is predicted to be 5.54 above week 11?">

No. It is the average extra signups per week across the whole fitted line, a
trend over all twelve points. The jump from week 11 to week 12 is 40, which
is the naive number. The slope is what gets multiplied by the horizon when you
forecast, so being clear about what it summarizes matters for everything below.

</Checkpoint>

## How good is the fit?

**A line can be drawn through anything. How do you know whether this one
describes the data?** Compare two squared totals. The spread before fitting
(each value against the average of all values) is `ssTot`; the spread left over
after fitting (each value against the line) is `ssRes`. R squared is
$$R^2 = 1 - ssRes / ssTot$$, the share of the spread the line explains: 1 means
a perfect fit, 0 means no better than always guessing the average.

| Fit                     | R squared | What it says                                |
| ----------------------- | --------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| Line through 12 weeks   | 0.606     | about 40% of the spread is left unexplained |
| Line through weeks 1-10 | 0.944     | the pre-viral weeks follow a line closely   |

The 12-week fit is not terrible, but the unexplained 40% sits where the viral
weeks are. A high R squared on weeks 1-10 describes steady growth of about 2
signups a week; it says nothing yet about whether the viral weeks will repeat.

<Checkpoint question="The 10-week fit has R squared 0.944 and the 12-week fit has 0.606. Which better describes the underlying weekly growth, and what does it still not tell you?">

The 10-week fit, because the line explains nearly all of the movement in those
weeks, while the 12-week line leaves much unexplained once the viral weeks join.
But a high R squared only measures fit inside the data it was fitted on. It does
not say the next forty weeks will keep following the line, which is the horizon
question in the next section.

</Checkpoint>

## How far out can a forecast be trusted?

**Two lines fitted to nearly the same data disagree by 30.8 signups at week 13. Why does the disagreement reach 167.4 at week 52?** Because each forecast is
the slope times the week number, so a difference in slope is multiplied by the
horizon. The gap between the two lines is
(5.54 - 2.04) x week + (13.67 - 28.40), which grows in a straight line.

| Week | 12-week line | Weeks 1-10 line | Gap   |

| 20   | 124.4        | 69.1            | 55.3  |
| 30   | 179.8        | 89.5            | 90.3  |
| 52   | 301.7        | 134.3           | 167.4 |

Inside weeks 1-12 the lines agree reasonably; beyond them they fan apart, and
neither is checked by any data. A line also has no ceiling or floor. Suppose, as an
assumption for illustration only, that the product's whole reachable audience can
produce at most about 150 signups a week. Then 302 is impossible and even 134 is a
guess about when growth stops. Straight-line extrapolation fails whenever
something the line cannot see changes: a ceiling, a campaign ending, a
competitor, a season turning.

<Checkpoint question="Two forecasts differ by 30.8 at week 13. Without refitting anything, roughly how large will the gap be at week 26 if both lines stay straight?">

Larger than 30.8, and you can compute it. The gap is a straight line itself:
(5.54 - 2.04) x week - 14.73, so at week 26 it is about 3.50 x 26 - 14.73 = 76.3.
(Check at week 13: 3.50 x 13 - 14.73 = 30.8.) The gap keeps growing because
the slope difference is multiplied by the week number, so disagreement between
two reasonable fits is itself a measure of how uncertain a distant forecast is.

</Checkpoint>

## What this worked example does and does not cover

This is one case, not the whole territory. The numbers are specific: one series,
one spike near the end, one straight-line model. Try changing it yourself.
What would the 12-week slope do if the viral post had come in week 3 instead of
week 12? What would change if every fourth week had a maintenance-window dip? And if the
spike were not a one-off but the start of a new level, would the pre-spike line
be the honest forecast or the stale one? Answering these means deciding what
kind of change the data contains, which no fit can decide for you.

The slider in L1 explores the first question's cousin: how much the 12-week
forecast moves as the spike size changes.

## Failure modes

| Failure mode                               | What it gets wrong                                                                  |
| ------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Reporting a forecast without its R squared | a low value means most of the variation is unexplained                              |
| Reading a fit's slope as the latest change | the slope averages across all points; the last jump is a different number           |
| Projecting as far as the line reaches      | the further out, the more the forecast depends on assumptions the data never tested |
| Using a line for a repeating pattern       | the slope can be flat or even negative while the real trend rises                   |
