Your team can run a risky database migration today, during business
hours, or wait three weeks for the next scheduled maintenance window.
Today, there's a 15% chance it triggers an outage that costs the
company $50,000 in lost orders and emergency fixes — but if it goes
fine, shipping now is worth $8,000 in engineering time saved and a
feature landing two weeks sooner. Waiting drops the outage risk to
2%, because the maintenance window has read-only fallbacks and a
support team on standby — but coordinating that window itself costs
something, so success is only worth $5,000.

| Option          | If it fails               | If it succeeds           |
| --------------- | ------------------------- | ------------------------ |
| Migrate now     | 15% chance, costs $50,000 | 85% chance, worth $8,000 |
| Wait for window | 2% chance, costs $50,000  | 98% chance, worth $5,000 |

**Your gut says "2% is obviously safer than 15%, just wait." But
"safer" isn't the same question as "which choice is worth more, on
average, given both the size of the risk and the size of the
reward?" Which option actually comes out ahead once both numbers are
weighed together, not just the probabilities?**

## The idea in one line

**Expected value (EV)** is the average outcome you'd see if you could
replay the same risky decision an enormous number of times — each
possible outcome counted in proportion to how often it happens.

```text
EV = (probability of outcome 1 × value of outcome 1)
   + (probability of outcome 2 × value of outcome 2)
   + ...
```

Applied to the migration decision:

| Option          | Calculation                         | Expected value |
| --------------- | ----------------------------------- | -------------- |
| Migrate now     | `0.15 × (−$50,000) + 0.85 × $8,000` | **−$700**      |
| Wait for window | `0.02 × (−$50,000) + 0.98 × $5,000` | **+$3,900**    |

Waiting isn't just the "safer-feeling" choice — it's worth about
$4,600 more, on average, than migrating today. That gap is the whole
point of doing the arithmetic instead of trusting "2% sounds fine."

## Key terms

- **Outcome** — one possible result of a decision (success, failure,
  partial success — anything mutually exclusive and collectively
  exhaustive for that decision).
- **Probability** — how likely each outcome is, written as a fraction
  between 0 and 1 (15% = 0.15). All the probabilities for one
  decision's outcomes must add up to 1.
- **Value** — what that outcome is actually worth, in whatever unit
  matters (dollars here, but it could be hours saved, users retained,
  or any comparable number). A cost is a negative value.
- **Expected value (EV)** — the probability-weighted average of every
  outcome's value. Not a guess at what will happen this one time — a
  long-run average across many repeats of the same bet.
- **Risk vs. expected value** — two decisions can have the same EV
  and completely different risk profiles (a guaranteed $100 vs. a 1%
  chance at $10,000). This unit's L2 covers why EV alone can still
  miss something that matters.

EV is a tool for comparing options **on average, across many similar
decisions over time** — not a forecast of a single roll. A team that
makes EV-positive calls consistently comes out ahead over a year of
decisions, even though any individual call can still go the "wrong"
way.

## What this unit covers

L2 builds the decision as a tree — the standard way to lay out a
choice with multiple branching outcomes — and covers where expected
value alone can mislead you (a single catastrophic outcome, even a
rare one, can matter more than the average suggests). L3 writes real,
runnable code: a general expected-value calculator, a decision-tree
evaluator, and a Monte Carlo simulation that confirms the two
options' EVs by actually "replaying" the decision thousands of times.
