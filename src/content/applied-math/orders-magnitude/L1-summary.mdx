An engineer estimates daily storage for a new logging pipeline: 2,000,000 records a day, each
roughly 5 KB. They multiply: `2,000,000 × 5 = 10,000,000`, label it "MB," and provision
storage for roughly 10 terabytes a day — an expensive, high-tier storage plan gets approved
on that number. **The actual number, computed correctly, was 10 gigabytes a day — one
thousand times smaller.** Nobody caught it in review, because "10,000,000 of something"
sounded appropriately large for "2 million records," and nobody stopped to ask whether the
_unit_ on that number was actually right.

The arithmetic (`2,000,000 × 5 = 10,000,000`) was completely correct. **The error was
entirely in the unit** — `2,000,000 records × 5 KB/record` produces a result measured in
KB, not MB, and treating a KB figure as an MB figure inflates it by exactly 1,000x, since
1 MB = 1,000 KB.

- **An order of magnitude is a power of 10** — roughly "how many zeros." Two numbers are
  "the same order of magnitude" if they're within about 10x of each other; a 1,000x gap
  (like the incident above) spans **three** orders of magnitude, not one — a much bigger
  miss than "off by a bit."
- **Dimensional analysis means tracking the unit through a calculation as carefully as the
  number itself** — units combine and cancel algebraically, exactly like variables:
  `records × (KB/record) = KB`, not "records" and not some unitless quantity. Losing track
  of what unit a result is actually in is precisely how the incident's `10,000,000` turned
  into "10,000,000 MB" instead of the correct "10,000,000 KB."
- **The fastest way to catch an error like this isn't redoing the exact calculation — it's
  a rough, independent order-of-magnitude sanity check.** "2 million records, each a few
  KB — that's a few times 10⁶ records times a few times 10³ bytes, so roughly 10⁹ to 10¹⁰
  bytes, which is around low tens of gigabytes" takes seconds and would have caught the 1,000x
  inflation instantly, without needing the exact figure.
- **Only quantities with the same unit can be added or subtracted; multiplication and
  division combine units freely** — `5 KB + 3 MB` isn't meaningful until converted to a
  shared unit first, but `2,000,000 records × 5 KB/record` is always meaningful, and its
  resulting unit (KB) is determined by the units being multiplied, not chosen freely.
- The practical skill this unit builds: before trusting any calculated number — cost,
  capacity, latency, storage — check both that its order of magnitude feels plausible from
  an independent rough estimate, and that its unit was actually tracked correctly through
  every step, not just assumed at the end.

## Orders of magnitude, at a glance

Powers of ten are just compact names for repeated zeros:

| Power | Ordinary number   | Plain-language size        |
| ----- | ----------------- | -------------------------- |
| 10³   | 1,000             | one thousand               |
| 10⁶   | 1,000,000         | one million                |
| 10⁹   | 1,000,000,000     | one billion                |
| 10¹²  | 1,000,000,000,000 | one trillion in US English |

This unit uses decimal data-unit conversions for clarity: `1 KB = 1,000 bytes`,
`1 MB = 1,000 KB`, `1 GB = 1,000 MB`, and `1 TB = 1,000 GB`. Some computing
contexts use binary units based on 1,024; that distinction matters in storage
systems, but it is not the source of the 1,000x incident here.

| Quantity                         | Approximate order of magnitude           |
| -------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------- |
| A single record (~5 KB)          | 10³ bytes                                |
| A daily log file (10 GB)         | 10¹⁰ bytes                               |
| The mistaken estimate (10 TB)    | 10¹³ bytes                               |
| Gap between correct and mistaken | 10³ = 1,000x — three orders of magnitude |

This isn't a trivia table — it's the actual sanity-check calculation that would have caught
the incident: comparing the reported number's order of magnitude against an independent,
rough estimate, rather than trusting a number just because the arithmetic that produced it
was correct.
