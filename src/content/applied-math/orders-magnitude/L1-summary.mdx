Someone says: "Counting out loud to a million is quick. A billion is only a
little more." Count one number every second, with no sleep and no breaks, and
ignore that long numbers take longer to say. **How long does it take to reach a
thousand, a million, and a billion?**

Most people guess the three answers sit close together. They do not. A thousand
takes about 17 minutes, a million takes about 12 days, and a billion takes
about 32 years. Each time you add three zeros, the time grows by a factor of
1,000.

## What does "an order of magnitude" actually count?

**If a million and a billion both just sound "huge", how do you tell them
apart?** Count the zeros. An **order of magnitude** is one power of ten. Two
numbers are "the same order of magnitude" when they are within about 10x of
each other, and each extra factor of 10 is one more order.

| Power | Ordinary number | Time to count to it, at 1 number per second |
| ----- | --------------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| 10³   | 1,000           | about 17 minutes                            |
| 10⁶   | 1,000,000       | about 12 days                               |
| 10⁹   | 1,000,000,000   | about 32 years                              |
| 10¹²  | 1 trillion      | about 31,700 years                          |

Going from a thousand to a billion is six orders of magnitude, a factor of
1,000,000, not "a bit more". Seeing that needs a log scale, where every grid
line is another factor of 10. On this chart the computer counting 1,000 numbers
per second is a flat 1,000x faster, so its line sits exactly three grid lines
below the human one, at every size.

## Can the same digits mean two very different amounts?

**Is `10,000,000` big?** It depends on what it is counting. Take a made-up
logging pipeline: 2,000,000 records a day, each about 5 KB. Multiplying gives
`2,000,000 × 5 = 10,000,000`. The arithmetic is right. The only open question is
the unit, and it moves the answer by three orders of magnitude.

| If the unit is...  | `10,000,000` of it is... | Verdict                        |
| ------------------ | ------------------------ | ------------------------------ |
| KB (what it is)    | 10 GB per day            | A modest, ordinary log volume  |
| MB (a mislabel)    | 10 TB per day            | A huge, expensive storage bill |
| Ratio between them | 1,000x                   | Three orders of magnitude      |

Nobody notices because `10,000,000` "sounds appropriately large" for 2 million
records. The unit that a calculation produces is not a label added at the end;
it comes out of the units you multiplied. The skill this unit builds has two
halves:

- **Dimensional analysis** — track the unit through every step, canceling units
  the way you cancel algebra terms. `records × KB/record` leaves KB.
- **Order-of-magnitude estimating** — rebuild the answer roughly, from scratch,
  and compare. "2 million times a few thousand bytes is about 10 billion bytes,
  about 10 GB" takes seconds and flags a "10 TB" report immediately.

This unit uses decimal units: 1 KB = 1,000 bytes, 1 MB = 1,000 KB, 1 GB = 1,000
MB, 1 TB = 1,000 GB. Binary units (1,024) differ by a few percent, which is far
smaller than the 1,000x slip here.

Only quantities in the same unit can be added; multiplying and dividing combine
units freely. `5 KB + 3 MB` is meaningless until one is converted, while
`2,000,000 records × 5 KB/record` is always meaningful and its unit is decided
by the factors, not chosen afterwards.
