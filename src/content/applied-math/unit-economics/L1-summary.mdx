## What "unit economics" means

Unit economics is the discipline of attaching a real dollar cost (and dollar revenue) to **one unit** of whatever you sell or ship — one user, one request, one transaction, one API call — instead of reasoning about a feature's cost only in the aggregate ("the cloud bill went up a bit").

| Term             | Question it answers                                                          |
| ---------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Fixed cost       | What do we pay regardless of volume?                                         |
| Variable cost    | What do we pay per additional unit?                                          |
| Cost per unit    | What does _one_ unit actually cost, once fixed cost is spread across volume? |
| Break-even point | How many units before revenue catches up to cost?                            |
| Margin           | Of the revenue one unit brings in, how much is left after its cost?          |

## The two cost types

Every real cost falls into one of two buckets, and mixing them up is where most "shouldn't cost much" guesses go wrong:

- **Fixed cost** — doesn't change with how many units you produce this month. The $3,200 GPU reservation is fixed: it's the same bill whether 1 suggestion or 1,000,000 suggestions get generated.
- **Variable cost** — scales directly with volume. The $0.011 per suggestion is variable: generate twice as many, pay twice as much.

Total cost for a period is always `fixed + (variable per unit × units)`. Cost **per unit** divides that total back down: `fixed / units + variable per unit`. That division is exactly why cost-per-unit falls as volume grows — the same $3,200 gets spread over more units — and exactly why a low-volume segment (like the free tier's 40,000 users generating comparatively few units _each_, but many units _in total_) can look cheap per user while still being expensive in aggregate.

## Amortization, briefly

Amortization is the same idea applied over **time** instead of volume: spreading a lump-sum cost (a yearly contract, a one-time migration cost) evenly across the months it covers, so no single month's numbers are distorted by a cost that actually belongs to the whole year. L2 covers the schedule mechanics; L3 has a runnable function for it.

## Break-even and margin

Two questions close the loop once cost-per-unit is known:

- **Break-even**: at what volume does total revenue equal total cost? Below that volume, the feature loses money; above it, it turns a profit.
- **Margin**: for one unit sold, how much of its revenue survives after subtracting its cost? A paid user generating 60 suggestions/month costs roughly **$0.73** (their share of fixed cost plus their variable usage) against **$12** of revenue — a healthy margin. A free user generating 25 suggestions costs roughly **$0.34** against **$0** of revenue — pure loss, at any volume.

These aren't abstract finance terms here — they're the exact arithmetic that would have caught the reply-suggestions problem before the bill did, not after.
