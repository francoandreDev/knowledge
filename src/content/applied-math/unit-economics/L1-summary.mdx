You pay **$20** for a spot on the corner, then make lemonade at **$0.50** a cup and sell
it for **$2.00**. A slow afternoon: you sell 10 cups. A busy one: 40. You made the same
lemonade both days, so why does a cup feel cheap on one day and a money-loser on the other?
**What does one cup actually cost you?**

## Which costs stay put, and which grow with every cup?

**The stall fee is $20 whether you sell 1 cup or 100. The lemons are not.** That split is
the whole foundation.

| Term          | Question it answers                               | Lemonade stand example |
| ------------- | ------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------- |
| Fixed cost    | What do I pay no matter how much I sell?          | $20 stall fee          |
| Variable cost | What do I pay for each extra unit?                | $0.50 ingredients      |
| Cost per unit | What does one unit cost, all-in?                  | depends on volume      |
| Break-even    | How many units until revenue covers cost?         | L2                     |
| Margin        | How much of each sale is left after its own cost? | L2                     |

Total cost for a day is always `fixed + variable per unit × units`. At 10 cups that is
$20 + $5 = $25. At 40 cups it is $20 + $20 = $40.

## Why does one cup get cheaper on a busy day?

**Spread the stall fee over more cups and each one carries less of it.** Divide the total
back down:

`cost per cup = fixed ÷ cups + variable per cup`

| Cups sold | Fixed share per cup | Plus ingredients | Cost per cup |
| --------- | ------------------- | ---------------- | ------------ |
| 10        | $2.00               | $0.50            | $2.50        |
| 20        | $1.00               | $0.50            | $1.50        |
| 40        | $0.50               | $0.50            | $1.00        |
| 100       | $0.20               | $0.50            | $0.70        |

At 10 cups you sell for $2.00 something that costs $2.50 to make, so every cup loses
money. The cost per cup never falls below the $0.50 ingredients, because that part does
not shrink with volume.

## The same arithmetic, bigger bill

A team ships "reply suggestions", a feature that drafts quick replies, to every user,
free tier included, because it "shouldn't cost much." The bill has one fixed line and one
variable line (all figures here are round-number assumptions, not any vendor's real
price list):

| Line              | Amount                                 |
| ----------------- | -------------------------------------- |
| Reserved GPU pool | $3,200 per month (fixed)               |
| Each suggestion   | $0.011 (variable)                      |
| Free tier         | 40,000 users x 25 suggestions, pays $0 |
| Paid tier         | 8,000 users x 60 suggestions, pays $12 |

Total: $3,200 + 1,480,000 suggestions x $0.011 = **$19,480 per month**. "Shouldn't cost
much" was a guess. The free tier alone costs about **$13,667** a month and brings in
nothing. L2 shows how that loss hides inside a healthy-looking average, and how to find
it with break-even and margin.
