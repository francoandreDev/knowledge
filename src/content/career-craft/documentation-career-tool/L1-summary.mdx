An engineer spends a Saturday tracking down and fixing a production
bug that had been silently corrupting a small percentage of
customer records for weeks — quietly, without asking for credit,
because "the work should speak for itself." Six months later, at
promotion time, their manager sits down to write the case for
promotion and can clearly recall a flashy demo from two months ago,
but not the bug fix, the mentoring conversations, or the three
smaller reliability improvements shipped in between. The promotion
packet ends up thin. **The engineer did the work. The work was real
and valuable. Why did it barely make it into the packet, and whose
fault is that — the manager's memory, or something the engineer could
have controlled?**

</Scenario>

## The shape of the problem

- Nobody — not even a good manager — reliably remembers six months
  of someone else's work in enough detail to make a strong case for
  them. Memory favors what's recent, dramatic, or personally
  witnessed, not what was quietly, competently handled.
- "The work should speak for itself" assumes someone is listening
  closely enough, for long enough, to hear it — which is rarely true
  once six months and a dozen other people's work are competing for
  the same manager's attention.
- **Documentation as a career tool** means keeping your own running
  record of what you did and why it mattered — not to replace doing
  the work, but so the record of it doesn't depend entirely on
  someone else's memory.

## Key terms

- **Brag doc** — a running, personal log of work completed and its
  impact, updated regularly (not written from scratch at review
  time) so nothing has to be reconstructed from memory later.
- **Visibility** — the degree to which the people who make decisions
  about your career (promotion, raises, opportunities) actually know
  what you've done — distinct from whether the work itself was good.
- **Self-promotion anxiety** — the discomfort of documenting your own
  work, often rooted in a fear that stating facts about your own
  impact will be read as boasting.
- **Objective framing** — describing impact in terms of what
  happened and what changed ("reduced incident response time from 40
  minutes to 8"), rather than subjective self-assessment ("I did a
  great job on this").

## What this unit covers

L2 works through why objective framing resolves the tension between
visibility and bragging — and what specifically distinguishes a brag
doc entry that reads as advocacy from one that reads as analysis.
L3 walks a real brag doc entry through two drafts: the anxious first
attempt that either overclaims or under-describes the work, and the
objective-framing rewrite that a manager could lift directly into a
promotion packet without editing.
