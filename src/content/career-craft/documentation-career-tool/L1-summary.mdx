An engineer spends a Saturday tracking down and fixing a production bug
that had been quietly damaging a small share of customer records for weeks.
They ask for no credit, because "the work should speak for itself."

Six months later, at promotion time, the manager sits down to write the
case. They clearly recall a flashy demo from two months ago. They cannot
recall the bug fix, the mentoring conversations, or the three smaller
reliability improvements. The packet comes out thin.

**The work was real and valuable. Why did it barely reach the packet, and
whose job was it to prevent that: the manager's memory, or something the
engineer could have controlled?**

## The shape of the problem

- Nobody reliably remembers six months of someone else's work in enough
  detail to argue for it. Memory keeps what is recent, dramatic, or
  personally witnessed, not what was handled quietly and well.
- "The work should speak for itself" assumes someone is listening closely
  enough, for long enough, to hear it. That rarely survives six months of
  competing work.
- **Documentation as a career tool** means keeping your own running record
  of what you did and why it mattered, so the record does not depend on
  someone else's memory.

The same six months, laid out side by side:

| What happened over six months          | Recalled by the manager unaided? |
| -------------------------------------- | -------------------------------- |
| Saturday production bug fix (silent)   | No                               |
| Three smaller reliability improvements | No                               |
| Several mentoring conversations        | No                               |
| A flashy demo, two months before       | Yes                              |

One recent, visible event out of four real contributions survived. The
other three did not matter less. They were simply quiet.

## Key terms

- **Brag doc**: a running, personal log of work completed and its impact,
  updated close to when the work happens rather than written from scratch
  at review time.
- **Visibility**: how well the people who decide about your career know
  what you did. It is separate from whether the work was good.
- **Self-promotion anxiety**: the discomfort of writing about your own
  impact, often from a fear that stating facts will read as boasting.
- **Objective framing**: describing what happened and what changed ("cut
  incident response from 40 minutes to 8" in an illustrative case) instead
  of grading yourself ("I did a great job").

## What this unit covers

L2 explains why objective framing dissolves the tension between visibility
and bragging, what an entry needs to contain, and how a running log is
kept. L3 takes the opening Saturday bug fix through two weak drafts and one
strong rewrite, then asks how the pattern works for work with no clean
number.
