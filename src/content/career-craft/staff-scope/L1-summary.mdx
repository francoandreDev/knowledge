Marcus Chen is an engineer on the Payments team at Northwind Commerce, and
a promotion to staff engineer is on the table this cycle. While chasing a
routine bug, he notices that Payments retries failed calls to Meridian Pay,
the payment vendor. So do Fulfillment's shipping-cost calculator and
Search's pricing service. Three teams wrote three separate retry
implementations, and none of them is safe to repeat. When Meridian times
out and a retry fires anyway, the customer is charged twice. Last quarter
that cost the company $40,000 in refunds.

Marcus could ship a clean, correct fix for Payments by Friday. Nobody
would question it in review.

**If he does, has he done the most valuable thing available to him, or only
the fastest thing within his own team's walls?**

## What does "scope" actually measure?

**Is the retry bug hard?** Not especially. Marcus could fix it alone in days.
So what separates this bug from the ones he fixes every week?

|                      | Owning your team's outcomes    | Owning outcomes across teams                                  |
| -------------------- | ------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------- |
| The problem lives in | One team's code and roadmap    | Several teams' code, none of whose roadmaps will fix it alone |
| Who has to act       | You and your teammates         | Engineers on teams that do not report to you                  |
| Your main tool       | Your own implementation skill  | Technical direction: steering decisions others will carry out |
| Bounded by           | Your own hours                 | How many teams adopt the approach                             |
| Retry bug, seen as   | Three separate team-sized bugs | One pattern with one shared cause                             |

- **Scope** is how many teams' decisions your judgment is expected to
  shape. It does not measure how hard the work is.
- **Technical direction** means steering a decision that other people will
  implement, instead of implementing it yourself.
- Being the best engineer on your own team does not by itself produce either
  one. It is a different shape of ownership, not a higher grade of the same
  one.

## Why can the correct fix still be the wrong move?

**Nothing is wrong with Marcus's Friday fix.** So why hesitate?

```mermaid
flowchart LR
    B["Same bug,\nfound once"] --> F["Friday fix\nPayments only"]
    B --> D["Shared approach\nthree teams adopt it"]
    F --> F2["1 of 3 teams fixed\nthe other two rediscover it"]
    D --> D2["All 3 teams fixed\nthe pattern is closed"]
```

The question a cross-team decision turns on is narrower than "what is the
correct fix". It is "whose problem is this at the scope I am responsible
for, and what is the highest-leverage way to close it there?"

- **Org-level leverage** means multiplying an outcome across teams instead
  of producing it once, yourself, for one team.
- A fix can be technically excellent and still aimed at a third of the
  problem.
- That does not make quick fixes bad. A narrow, urgent problem with no
  owner still deserves the keyboard (L2 covers when).

## What is the job's real output?

Two further consequences get their own levels. **Saying no** to legitimate
work that does not move the needle at your scope is how attention stays
pointed at the large problems. **Sponsoring** someone else, by handing them
a visible, growth-shaped piece of the work, multiplies output without
adding hours. L2 builds the model behind both, and L3 walks one quarter of
Marcus's case move by move.
