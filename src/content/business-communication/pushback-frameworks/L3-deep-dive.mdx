**Before reading the three versions below, predict: of the two bad
options — flat refusal and silent overcommitment — which one do you think
tends to damage a working relationship faster over repeated instances,
and why might that not be the more "obviously rude" one?**

</Scenario>

## Version 1: flat refusal

> "No, I can't take that on right now, the sprint's full."

Dana has no way to evaluate this. Is the sprint genuinely at capacity, or
is Marcus just not prioritizing it? She can't offer to move something
else, because she doesn't know what "full" actually consists of. If this
happens more than once, Dana starts to read Marcus as uncooperative,
whether or not the constraint was real.

## Version 2: silent overcommitment

> "Sure, I'll get it in by Friday."

This feels smooth in the moment — Dana walks away happy. But Marcus now
has three commitments crammed into the same week with no plan for which
one slips. Most likely, either the billing bug fix ships late (discovered
only when it's already overdue), or the export feature itself is rushed
and buggy at the demo. Either way, the actual cost of this "yes" surfaces
later, as a broken commitment Dana never agreed to.

## Version 3: trade-off pushback

> "I can take this on — but the sprint's already committed to the billing
> bug fix and the migration, both due this week too. If the export
> feature is the priority, I can ship it by Friday and push the migration
> to early next week instead. Or, if the migration timeline is firm, I
> can get the export feature out by Tuesday of next week. Which matters
> more for the demo?"

This names the real constraint (two specific existing commitments, not a
vague "I'm busy"), offers two concrete trade-offs (swap the migration's
timeline, or push the export feature's date), and hands the actual
priority call back to Dana — who's the only one who knows whether the
demo date or the migration timeline actually matters more.

## What changed, concretely

| Element                    | Version 1                        | Version 2                                   | Version 3                               |
| -------------------------- | -------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------- |
| Constraint named?          | Vaguely ("the sprint's full")    | Not at all                                  | Yes — two specific existing commitments |
| Options offered?           | None                             | None (implicitly: everything)               | Two concrete, named trade-offs          |
| Who decides the trade-off? | Nobody — request just declined   | Nobody — cost surfaces later, unchosen      | Dana — with the real cost visible       |
| Where does the cost land?  | On the relationship, ambiguously | On whichever commitment breaks first, later | Explicitly agreed upon in advance       |

## When the requester has more positional power than you

**Everything above assumed Dana and Marcus are peers who can negotiate
openly. What changes if the requester is a skip-level executive instead —
someone Marcus can't simply hand a decision back to the same way?** The
structure doesn't disappear, but the framing shifts: instead of "which do
you want me to prioritize?" (which can read as pushing a decision upward
inappropriately), it becomes "I want to flag the trade-off before
committing: taking this on means the migration slips to next week — I
wanted you to have that visibility before I confirm." The constraint and
trade-off are still named explicitly; what changes is that the responder
states the trade-off as information the requester needs, rather than
posing it as an open question — because with a large power gap, an open
question can be read as deflecting a decision that isn't really theirs to
push back.

## Extend it: what if there genuinely is no trade-off — the sprint really does have slack?

**Every example above assumed a real constraint existed. What should the
response be if, after actually checking, there's no meaningful trade-off
to surface — the request really can be absorbed without cost?** Saying so
plainly ("sure, I can fit this in without anything else slipping") is
itself informative and still worth stating explicitly rather than leaving
Dana to wonder whether a silent cost is being hidden — the trade-off
pushback framework isn't about inventing a constraint to seem
appropriately cautious; it's about surfacing whatever the real trade-off
actually is, including "none."

## Failure modes

| Failure mode                                                                   | What it gets wrong                                                                                                                                 |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Refusing without naming the actual constraint                                  | Requester can't evaluate or negotiate a decision they can't see the reasoning behind                                                               |
| Accepting without surfacing the real cost                                      | The trade-off still happens — it just lands later, unplanned, and reads as a broken commitment instead of an agreed trade                          |
| Offering vague flexibility instead of concrete options                         | "I'll try my best" gives the requester nothing specific to actually decide between                                                                 |
| Posing an open "which do you want?" to someone with much more positional power | Can read as pushing a decision back up inappropriately — better framed as surfaced information plus a stated plan                                  |
| Inventing a constraint that doesn't really exist, to seem appropriately busy   | Erodes trust once discovered, the same way overstating impact does in other feedback contexts — the framework depends on the constraint being real |
