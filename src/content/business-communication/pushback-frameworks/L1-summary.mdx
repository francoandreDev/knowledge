A product manager asks, "can you squeeze this in by Friday?" — on top of
a sprint that's already fully committed. Saying "no" outright feels
uncooperative. Saying "yes" without saying anything else guarantees
something breaks quietly later. **Is there a third option that's neither
a flat refusal nor a silent overcommitment?**

</Scenario>

## Plain-language setup

| Term                     | Meaning in this scenario                                                                |
| ------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Product manager          | The person coordinating product/customer priorities                                     |
| Sprint                   | A short period of planned work, often one or two weeks                                  |
| Fully committed          | The planned work already fills the available time; adding more means moving something   |
| Squeeze this in          | Add work without formally changing the plan                                             |
| Something breaks quietly | A promise slips, quality drops, someone works unplanned extra time, or a deadline moves |

The same pattern works outside software too. If you already promised
to study for an exam and someone asks you to organize an event the same
night, the honest answer is not just "yes" or "no"; it is "I can do
that only if something else changes."

## The shape of the problem

- A flat "no" answers the request but hides the actual reason, so the
  requester can't tell if it's a real constraint or just reluctance.
- A silent "yes" answers the relationship in the moment but transfers the
  entire cost of the trade-off onto whoever ends up missing something
  later, without them ever agreeing to that outcome.

## Key terms

- **Trade-off pushback** — instead of accepting or refusing a request
  outright, naming the real constraint and the concrete trade-off it
  creates, then handing the decision on how to resolve that trade-off
  back to the requester.
- **Silent overcommitment** — agreeing to a request without surfacing
  what it will cost, so the actual cost (a missed deadline, a rushed
  result, a burned weekend) shows up later, unplanned and unagreed-to.
- **Constraint** — the specific, concrete reason a request can't simply be
  added on top of everything else — not a vague feeling of being "too
  busy," but a stated fact the requester can evaluate.
- **Informed trade-off** — a decision the requester makes with the real
  cost visible up front, rather than one they discover only after
  something has already broken.

For a deeper decision-role version of trade-offs, connect this with
`business-communication/decision-making-frameworks`.

## What this unit covers

L2 works through why trade-off pushback preserves the relationship in a
way that neither a flat refusal nor a silent yes does, and what makes a
constraint concrete enough to be useful. L3 rewrites the Friday-deadline
scenario above three ways — blunt refusal, silent overcommitment, and
trade-off pushback — so the difference is visible in the actual words
used, not just described abstractly.
