Priya spent three weeks tracking down the real cause of an outage that
had taken down checkout three separate times, fixed it for good, and
posted a summary in a Slack thread that four people read. Marcus
shipped a smaller feature the same month, and mentioned it to his
manager Elena in their 1:1, in the team standup, and again in a
hallway conversation. At calibration — a closed-door meeting where
Elena has to argue for her team's promotions to other managers who
never watched either engineer work — she can describe Marcus's month
in detail. When another manager asks what Priya did this quarter,
Elena pauses, then gives a vaguer answer than the work deserved.
**Priya did the harder, more valuable thing. Why does Elena, who likes
and respects her, still make a weaker case for her?**

## The shape of the problem

- Work that never reaches the person who has to argue for you in a
  room you're not in might as well not have happened, for the purpose
  of that room — competence alone doesn't travel on its own.
- The instinctive fix — telling people what you did — creates a second
  problem: said too often, in the first person, without anything
  backing it up, it reads as self-promotion, and self-promotion costs
  social credibility even when every word of it is true.
- The real failure isn't "visible" versus "invisible." It's that most
  people only know two modes — stay quiet and hope the work speaks for
  itself, or speak up and risk sounding like they're campaigning — and
  both of those modes lose.

## Key terms

- **Visibility** — the degree to which people who make decisions about
  your work (promotion, staffing, priority) actually know it happened,
  independent of whether it was good.
- **Self-promotion** — declaring your own value directly, usually in
  the first person ("I did X," "I'm the reason Y worked") — the thing
  that reads as boastful when it's frequent, unprompted, or
  unsupported by anything the listener can check.
- **Evidence-based visibility** — making the work itself, or a record
  of it, visible instead of declaring your role in it verbally — a
  dashboard, a doc, a before/after number, a linked ticket.
- **Credit-sharing** — naming other people's specific contributions
  alongside your own, which paradoxically makes your own role _more_
  credible, not less, because it reads as accurate rather than
  self-serving.
- **Decision point** — a specific moment (a planning meeting, a
  calibration cycle, a postmortem review) when someone with authority
  is actively forming a judgment that your work is relevant to —
  visibility timed near one of these does far more than the same
  information shared at a random moment.

In plain words: the goal isn't "get people to notice me." It's "make
sure the true record of what I did is sitting somewhere a
decision-maker can find it, at the moment they're forming a judgment
that depends on it" — without that record's presence depending on you
personally announcing your own greatness.

## What this unit covers

L2 works out why "just do good work" fails as a strategy on its own —
the mechanism is that a manager or peer can only advocate for what
they can actually cite, not what they vaguely remember hearing was
good — and lays out four concrete tactics that create visibility
without reading as self-promotion. L3 walks through Priya's actual
next two quarters: the specific documentation, credit-sharing, and
timing choices that changed what Elena could say about her, worked
example by example, plus the failure modes that turn each tactic back
into either invisibility or boastfulness if done wrong.
