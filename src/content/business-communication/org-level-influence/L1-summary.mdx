Yuki is a principal engineer at Solstice Health. Two years ago the
company had 40 engineers and a handful of services; today it has 190
engineers across 14 teams, and the service boundaries still mostly
follow whichever team happened to write the first version of some
code — not the actual shape of the business. The result is services
that call each other in tangled, chatty ways, duplicated logic in
three places, and a production incident roughly every other month
where one team's "small" change silently breaks two others. Yuki is
convinced the company needs to draw service boundaries around real
business domains going forward — but there's no VP asking her for a
recommendation, no steering committee, and no meeting on the calendar
where this gets decided. It will get decided fourteen separate times,
by fourteen tech leads, over the next year, each time one of them
starts or restructures a service. **If there's no meeting to win and
no one person to convince, what does "getting this to happen" even
mean — and how is that a different problem from convincing one room
of people on one day?**

</Scenario>

It's a different problem entirely. Winning a single decision is a
sprint with a deadline; shaping company-wide direction is closer to
setting a policy that has to survive dozens of decisions you won't be
in the room for.

## Two different kinds of influence problem

- A **single-decision influence problem** has a clear moment: one
  meeting, one sign-off, a small number of named people whose minds
  need changing before a specific date. `informal-authority` covers
  this — mapping the real decision-makers, building a coalition
  before the meeting, and winning that one moment.
- An **org-level influence problem** has no such moment. The
  "decision" is really dozens of smaller decisions, made by different
  people, on different schedules, most of which Yuki will never be
  invited to. Winning it doesn't mean persuading a room — it means
  making her position the **default** people reach for when they
  aren't thinking about her at all.

|                           | Single-decision influence                       | Org-level influence                                           |
| ------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Shape of the decision     | One moment, one room, one outcome               | Many small decisions, over months, mostly without you present |
| What "winning" looks like | The room agrees on the day                      | Your position becomes the default other people reach for      |
| Main tool                 | A coalition built before the meeting            | Durable artifacts, reputation, and where you spend attention  |
| Time horizon              | Days to weeks                                   | Months to years                                               |
| How you show up           | In the room, in person, at the moment it counts | In writing, cited by people who never talked to you directly  |

Suppose Yuki gets an invite to present her service-boundary idea at
next month's all-hands architecture review — one slot, fifteen
minutes, in front of all 14 tech leads at once. Does a strong
fifteen-minute pitch solve her problem the way winning the migration
meeting solved Dev's, in `informal-authority`? Not on its own. A
single strong pitch can raise awareness, but the actual decisions
happen later, team by team, when a tech lead is heads-down designing
their own service and Yuki isn't in the room. Fifteen minutes in a
shared meeting doesn't put a citable artifact in front of that tech
lead at the exact moment they're deciding, and it doesn't build the
track record that makes them trust the recommendation months after
the talk is forgotten. The pitch is one input among several — not the
moment the decision gets made, because there isn't a single moment.

## The tools that work at this scale

- **Durable artifacts.** A conversation ends when the conversation
  ends. A written RFC, design doc, or architecture decision record
  (ADR) keeps working after Yuki stops talking about it — a tech lead
  designing a new service six months from now can find it, read it,
  and cite it to their own team without ever having spoken to Yuki.
- **Reputation built on track record.** Nobody defers to a stranger's
  opinion on company-wide technical direction. They defer to someone
  whose past calls turned out to be right, visibly, more than once.
  This compounds slowly and can't be shortcut by a single good
  presentation.
- **Seeding ideas before they're needed.** By the time a tech lead
  is actively deciding how to structure a new service, it's often too
  late to introduce a new idea from scratch. The idea needs to
  already be familiar — from a review comment, a hallway conversation,
  a doc they skimmed months earlier — so it feels like confirming
  something they'd already half-decided, not adopting someone else's
  agenda.
- **A finite influence budget.** Yuki has a real job, and only so many
  hours, review comments, and conversations she can spend being "the
  person who's right about architecture" before people stop reading
  what she writes. Spending that budget on every disagreement in the
  company — naming conventions, a test-framework preference, a minor
  API style choice — leaves nothing for the few bets that actually
  matter.

In plain words:

| Term                      | Simple meaning                                                                                                       |
| ------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Org-level influence       | Shaping many future decisions you won't be present for, not one you are                                              |
| Durable artifact          | A written RFC, design doc, or ADR that keeps making the case after the conversation ends                             |
| Reputation / track record | Trust built from being visibly right over time, not from a single strong argument                                    |
| Seeding                   | Introducing an idea before it's needed, so it's already familiar when a decision arrives                             |
| Influence budget          | The finite time and credibility a person has to spend being heard — not an infinite resource                         |
| Company-wide bet          | A specific, chosen direction worth spending real influence budget on, as opposed to every disagreement that comes up |

The rest of this unit works out how each tool actually operates —
what makes an artifact durable instead of just written, how
reputation compounds (and how it's spent, not just earned), how
seeding works in practice, and how Yuki decides which few bets are
worth her limited influence budget instead of trying to weigh in on
everything.
