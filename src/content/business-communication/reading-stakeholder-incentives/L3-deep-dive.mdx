## The first pitch, and exactly where it lost the room

> "We've identified that Tool A and Tool B do about 70% overlapping work. Consolidating
> onto Tool B would cut our maintenance burden by roughly a third and remove a whole
> category of sync bugs between the two systems. Here's the migration plan and timeline."

Technically flawless. Numbers hold up. And it's addressed to the room in general — nobody
is named as the one who benefits, and the team lead who built Tool A hears it as: _the
thing I shipped last year is being described as 70% redundant, in front of my peers,
right before review season._ Nothing in the pitch was false. Nothing in the pitch
addressed what that lead actually stood to lose by agreeing in front of everyone.

## Building the incentive map before trying again

Applying L2's four questions specifically to the team lead who rejected it:

| Question              | Answer for this stakeholder                                                       |
| --------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Measured on           | Their team's visible technical contributions this review cycle                    |
| Gains from yes        | Reduced maintenance load (real, but small and indirect)                           |
| Loses from yes        | Public framing of their team's flagship project as redundant, right before review |
| Timing risk right now | High — review season, this exact framing is costly at this exact moment           |

The "loses" row is doing all the work here. It's not that the lead disagreed with the
math — the rejection meeting never actually engaged with the math at all.

## The second pitch, built directly against that row

> "We'd like [Team A] to lead the consolidation — you know Tool A's internals better than
> anyone, and this positions the migration as an infrastructure improvement your team is
> driving, not something being done to your system. The cost savings and reduced sync
> bugs are real, but framed as _your team's_ efficiency initiative for this cycle, which
> is genuinely a strong review story: a whole class of bugs eliminated, maintenance
> burden cut, and you led the technical call on how it happened."

**Not one number changed.** The migration plan, the cost savings, the technical
architecture are all identical to the first pitch. What changed is who's positioned to
own the outcome and how it reads against the exact thing the incentive map identified as
at risk — the team lead's standing going into review. This pitch doesn't ask them to
agree their prior work was redundant; it hands them a new, better story about the same
underlying work.

## Extend the scenario: what if the timing genuinely can't move?

The reframing above worked because ownership could plausibly shift to the threatened
team. **What happens if the proposal specifically requires a team OTHER than the one
losing standing to lead it — reframing to give them ownership isn't a real option — and
the deadline can't wait until after review season either?** Trace it through: neither of
L2's two levers (timing, framing) is available here, which means the actual fix has to
be different — likely, making the _cost of saying no_ concrete and visible to whoever the
team lead answers to, so their own incentive calculus shifts (declining now has its own
cost, not just accepting does), or finding a genuinely smaller, lower-visibility first
step that doesn't trigger the same public "your work was redundant" framing, deferring
the full consolidation's optics to a less exposed moment. **The specific fix always
depends on which row of the incentive map is actually blocking — there's no single
technique that works regardless of which factor (gain, loss, timing) is doing the
blocking.**

**A second question worth sitting with:** what if the incentive map reveals the objection
isn't really about THIS proposal at all, but about a pattern — this is the third time this
team's projects have been proposed for consolidation or retirement? At that point the
real fix may not be about this pitch's framing at all, but a longer conversation about
how technical decisions in this org keep landing disproportionately on one team, which is
a different, larger problem than any single incentive map can solve.

## Failure modes

| Failure mode                                                                | What it gets wrong                                                                                                                                                        |
| --------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Assuming a rejected good idea means the decision-maker is being irrational  | The team lead's objection was entirely rational from their own position — dismissing it as irrationality skips the actual diagnosis                                       |
| Responding to rejection by making the technical case stronger               | The first pitch already won on technical merit; strengthening a case that was never the actual objection doesn't address the real blocker                                 |
| Treating incentive-mapping as manipulation                                  | The honest technical case doesn't change — what changes is understanding why an honest, correct case still failed, and addressing the real, legitimate concern underneath |
| Assuming the same fix (reframing ownership) works for every rejection       | The right lever depends on which row of the map is actually driving resistance — timing, framing, and cost-of-no are different tools for different blockers               |
| Stopping at "we identified the incentive" without changing the actual pitch | Understanding the incentive is diagnostic, not sufficient on its own — the second pitch still has to be built differently, not just internally understood differently     |
