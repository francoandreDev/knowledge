An engineer proposes merging two overlapping internal tools into one — cleaner
architecture, real maintenance savings, no technical downside anyone can point to. It gets
rejected. Not with a counter-argument about the numbers — just a firm "not right now" from
the lead of the team that owns the tool being retired. **The proposal was, by every
technical measure, obviously right. So why did it die in one meeting?**

The tool being retired was that lead's own team's flagship project from the previous year.
Approving the merge — right before performance review season — would read as an admission
that a year of their team's work was redundant. **The proposal was evaluated correctly on
its technical merits and rejected anyway, because technical merit was never the actual
criterion the decision was being made against.**

</Scenario>

- **A proposal isn't evaluated in a vacuum — it's evaluated against how it affects the
  decision-maker's own goals, incentives, and risks**, which are often invisible from
  outside their position. "This is obviously the right call" is a claim about the
  proposal's merit; it says nothing about whether accepting it helps or hurts the specific
  person who has to say yes.
- **Rejection of a good idea is information, not just an obstacle** — when a well-reasoned
  proposal gets turned down without a real counter-argument, that's usually a signal that
  something outside the visible facts (a competing incentive, a timing conflict, a risk to
  someone's standing) is doing the actual deciding, and it's worth identifying before
  trying again with the same pitch.
- **Incentives aren't the same as malice or irrationality** — the team lead in the scenario
  wasn't being unreasonable or acting in bad faith; from their position, protecting a
  major piece of their own team's recent work right before review season is a completely
  rational priority. Reading incentives means understanding this, not assuming
  stakeholders who disagree are simply wrong or difficult.
- **The visible case (cost, architecture, timeline) and the invisible case (what this
  costs or gains a specific person) are answering two different questions** — a proposal
  can win completely on the first and still lose, because the second question is the one
  actually being decided.
- The practical skill this unit builds: before pitching a proposal, ask not just "is this
  the right call" but **"what does saying yes cost this specific person, and what does
  saying no cost them"** — the answer often explains a rejection that otherwise looks
  irrational, and points directly at what needs to change for a second attempt to land.

## Two different scorecards

|                             | Visible scorecard (the pitch)        | Invisible scorecard (the decision)                               |
| --------------------------- | ------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| What's measured             | Cost, architecture quality, timeline | How this affects the decision-maker's standing, workload, credit |
| Who sees it                 | Everyone in the room                 | Usually only the decision-maker themselves                       |
| What the proposal addressed | Everything on this list              | Nothing on this list                                             |

The proposal won the left column completely and never engaged with the right one at all —
which is exactly why a technically sound pitch produced a rejection that felt, from
outside, like it made no sense.
