## Before the meeting: getting the message in order

Before Priya says anything to her team, what does she actually need to
have decided — beyond just "be honest"?

A message assembled in the moment, live, in front of a nervous team,
tends to wander into things Priya isn't actually ready to say. She spends
30 minutes before the meeting writing down four things:

1. **What's decided, in one plain sentence** — the merge is happening;
   the combined team will be 10 people, down from 15 today.
2. **What's not decided, stated just as plainly** — which specific 5
   roles, and who fills the 10 remaining spots on the merged team, is not
   yet finalized.
3. **The one honest thing she can say about impact** — she cannot promise
   anyone's role is safe, and she will not pretend otherwise, but she will
   share names the moment HR finalizes them, not a day later.
4. **A specific next check-in** — Friday, June 12th, nine days out. Not
   "soon," not "as soon as I know" — a calendar date, stated out loud, in
   the room.

Writing these down first matters for a specific reason: it's the
difference between reacting to whatever question lands hardest in the
moment and delivering a message Priya actually chose in advance. It also
means when someone asks "so is my job safe?" mid-meeting, she isn't
inventing an answer under pressure — she already decided what she can and
can't say.

## The meeting itself

Priya calls a 15-minute team meeting for 2pm, same day the calendar
invite was spotted. She sends a two-line heads-up an hour before: "Want
to grab everyone for 15 minutes at 2pm — there's a real update I want to
share directly rather than let it turn into a guessing game." No
euphemism, no "quick sync" — naming that something real is coming, so no
one walks in blindsided.

**Priya:** "I want to tell you directly what's happening rather than let
the calendar invite some of you noticed turn into speculation. Fintrail
is merging our team with the Ledger team into one team of 10. That part
is decided — it's happening. What's not decided yet is which 10 people
that ends up being, out of the 15 of us combined. HR is finalizing that,
and I'll have real names by Friday the 12th — nine days from today."

**An engineer, Dev:** "So... is my job safe?"

**Priya:** "I'm not going to tell you 'don't worry' when I genuinely
don't know that yet — that wouldn't be fair to you, and I'd rather you
trust what I tell you than feel briefly better today. What I can tell you
is this: I don't have a list, I haven't been asked to rank anyone, and
the moment I know anything, you'll know it the same day — not after
leadership, not after HR, the same day. I know that's not the answer you
wanted. It's the honest one."

**Another engineer, Lin:** "What happens between now and Friday?"

**Priya:** "Work continues as normal — I need the Q3 migration to stay on
track regardless of how the org chart shakes out, and honestly, staying
focused on real work is going to make the next nine days easier than
sitting and waiting would be. I'll check in with the whole team again on
Friday the 12th, whatever the news is that day. If anything changes
before then, I'm not sitting on it until the scheduled date — you'll hear
it as soon as I do."

Notice what each answer does before reading on: Priya names the decided
part without hedging, names the undecided part without minimizing it,
refuses the comforting lie Dev is implicitly asking for while still
answering with something true and useful, and turns Lin's open-ended
anxiety into a bounded date plus a concrete thing to focus on in the
meantime.

<Checkpoint question="Dev directly asks 'is my job safe?' Priya doesn't answer yes or no — she explains what she does and doesn't know instead. Why is that the right move, mechanically, rather than a dodge?">

Because Priya does not have the information to answer yes or no honestly
— no list exists yet. A yes would be a promise she has no standing to
make and might have to walk back in nine days, which would cost far more
trust than the discomfort of not knowing costs today. A no would be
needlessly alarming about something that isn't decided. What she gives
instead — no list exists, no ranking has happened, the news reaches Dev
the same day it reaches her — isn't a dodge of the question; it's the
most complete and accurate answer available, delivered as itself instead
of forced into a yes/no shape it doesn't fit. The distinction matters:
declining to promise an outcome you don't control is not the same as
declining to answer.

</Checkpoint>

## What Priya does in the nine days after

The team meeting is the start of the wait, not the end of it. What
happens in the following days determines whether the Friday check-in
lands as Priya keeping her word or as one more thing that slipped.

| Day         | What Priya does                                                                            | Why                                                                                                                          |
| ----------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Same day    | 1:1s with the two engineers who first spotted the calendar invite                          | They found this on their own; a private word closes the loop and reduces the sense of being managed via a group announcement |
| Day 2–3     | Keeps 1:1s and standups running exactly as scheduled, no cancellations                     | Cancelled routine meetings read as a signal something's being hidden, even when nothing new has happened                     |
| Day 5       | Sends a short written update: "No news yet — still on track for Friday, nothing's changed" | Silence between the meeting and the deadline is exactly where rumor re-fills the gap; a short "nothing new" note closes it   |
| Day 9 (Fri) | Delivers the actual outcome, in the same team-meeting format as day 1                      | Keeping the promised format as well as the promised date reinforces that Priya's word is reliable                            |

The day-5 update matters more than it looks like it should. Nothing
happened — that's exactly the point. A team that hears nothing for nine
straight days starts wondering if Friday will actually happen, or if it
will slip the way vague timelines often do. A short, low-effort "still on
track, nothing new" message costs Priya two minutes and directly prevents
that specific doubt from forming.

<Checkpoint question="On day 5, literally nothing has changed since the team meeting. Why does Priya send an update anyway, instead of saving the communication for when there's actually real news?">

Because "nothing has changed" is itself information the team doesn't
have unless Priya says it. Without the day-5 message, silence between day
1 and day 9 is ambiguous — it could mean nothing happened, or it could
mean something happened that Priya hasn't shared yet, and a team already
primed by a leaked calendar invite will tend to assume the second one.
The two-minute update isn't about delivering news; it's about keeping the
promise from day 1 visibly alive during the part of the wait where it
would otherwise be easiest to doubt.

</Checkpoint>

## Friday: the outcome, and the individual conversations after

On Friday, HR confirms the final list. Two of the five eliminated roles
are on Priya's team: one is a role that's being fully absorbed into the
Ledger team's structure (the person is safe, their title changes), and
one is a genuine layoff — an engineer named Sam, whose specialization
doesn't map onto the merged team's needs.

Priya does the layoff conversation with Sam privately, before the group
meeting, not folded into it — a specific, individual loss should never be
first delivered as part of a group announcement, where the person hears
it at the same moment as everyone else and has no privacy to react.
Immediately after, she runs the group meeting exactly as promised: what's
final, what it means structurally, and — because the news genuinely
affects morale beyond just the people leaving — what support exists
(severance details, a stated reference policy, time to process before
diving back into sprint planning).

For the eight people whose roles are safe, Priya still gives each a short
individual 1:1, not just the group meeting. **Before reading on: why does
"you're safe" need its own private conversation, and not just live inside
the group announcement everyone already heard together?**

A relief delivered anonymously in a group ("the following people are
unaffected...") lands as procedural. A relief delivered individually,
with a moment of actual eye contact and a "I know the last nine days were
hard to sit with, and I wanted to tell you directly" lands as being seen
as a person, not processed as a line item — and it matters because these
are the people Priya needs to stay engaged and trusting through the
team's next few disruptive months of actually merging two teams'
workflows.

<Checkpoint question="Sam's layoff conversation happens privately, before the group meeting. What specifically would go wrong if Priya instead just announced the final list to everyone at once and let Sam find out with the group?">

Sam would learn they've lost their role in the same moment as everyone
else, with no privacy to react, ask questions, or process it before
having to sit through the rest of the meeting in front of colleagues.
It also puts Priya in the position of delivering the single hardest,
most individually consequential piece of news as one line in a group
readout, which flattens it into something closer to an org-chart update
than what it actually is — a specific person's job ending. A private
conversation first gives Sam room to react honestly and gives Priya room
to actually talk with them, not just at them, before the group ever
convenes.

</Checkpoint>

## Extend it: what changes if the details are different

The sequence above worked because Priya had a genuine decided fact to
anchor on (the merge itself), a real date she could commit to and keep,
and leadership that let her communicate early rather than sitting on it
until HR was fully done. Before moving on, work through what would
actually change — not just get harder — in two different versions of this
same problem:

**What if Priya had to deliver this to a much larger group** — say, a
40-person department instead of an 8-person team, in an all-hands format
instead of a small room? The four moves (known vs. unknown, no false
reassurance, a concrete date, naming the personal-impact question) still
apply, but the individual follow-up — the private 1:1 with Sam, the
personal "you're safe" conversations — doesn't scale the same way across
40 people in the same afternoon. What would need to change about the
_format_ of the follow-up, not the content of the message, to still give
people something other than a line item in a mass email?

**What if the news were still worse — not a merge with some roles safe,
but a full team dissolution with everyone's role ending on the same
date?** The four moves still hold, but "name the personal-impact
question" changes shape: there's no longer a genuine unknown to
acknowledge — the answer to "what does this mean for me" is now the same
answer for everyone, and known immediately. Does that make the
conversation easier or harder than Priya's actual one, where real
uncertainty had to be sat with for nine days — and what does "give a
concrete next check-in" even mean once there's no more decision left to
wait for?

Neither question has one clean answer — they're worth sitting with,
because the four moves are a starting shape for uncertain, disruptive
news in general, not a script that reproduces Priya's exact nine days in
every situation change management shows up in.
