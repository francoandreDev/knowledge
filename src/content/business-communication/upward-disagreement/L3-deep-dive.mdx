## Before the conversation: getting the evidence in order

Before Maya says anything to Marcus, what does she actually need to have
ready — beyond just "a bad feeling about the plan"?

A concern that arrives as a chart survives contact with a busy VP; a
concern that arrives as a feeling doesn't. Maya spends half a day turning
her load test into something she can put in front of Marcus in under two
minutes:

1. **The number itself** — the exact throughput where p99 latency crosses
   the SLA threshold (7,200 transactions per minute), not a vague "it
   struggles under load."
2. **The comparison that makes it matter** — last year's actual Black
   Friday peak (12,000 TPM), so the gap isn't abstract.
3. **A concrete alternative**, not just an objection — Maya has already
   sketched a phased cutover (settle 20% of merchant traffic on the new
   ledger first, watch it for 48 hours, then ramp) that keeps the board
   date roughly intact without betting the whole weekend on an unproven
   ceiling.
4. **The ask, stated plainly** — she isn't asking Marcus to cancel the
   migration. She's asking for 15 minutes to walk through one number
   before the plan locks further.

Bringing a proposed alternative alongside the objection matters for a
reason beyond politeness: it changes what Marcus is being asked to do.
Without one, Maya is asking him to absorb bad news and figure out what to
do about it alone. With one, she's asking him to evaluate a specific
choice — which is a much smaller, much less threatening ask.

## The conversation itself

Maya messages Marcus directly: "Got 15 minutes today or tomorrow? Found
something in the load test I want to walk through with you before the
cutover plan locks further." No mention of "problem" or "risk" in the
message itself — those words in a scheduling ping tend to make the other
person brace before they've even sat down.

They meet privately, in Marcus's office, no one else present.

**Maya:** "I want this cutover to hit the board date as much as you do —
that's actually why I wanted to grab you. I ran the full load test
against last year's Black Friday numbers, and I found something I think
we should look at together before the weekend locks in."

**Marcus:** "Okay — what'd you find?"

**Maya:** "The new ledger service is completely solid at normal volume.
But past about 7,200 transactions a minute, p99 latency blows through the
SLA and settlement requests start timing out. Last year's Black Friday
peak was 12,000 — so we'd be running the new system about 60% into its
tested ceiling on day one, with no rollback path once the cutover starts."

**Marcus:** "The vendor benchmarks said it'd handle our full volume."

**Maya:** "That's what I thought too, going in — this is our own load
test against our own traffic pattern, not the vendor's synthetic
benchmark, so I trust the number, but I wanted you to see it yourself
before it goes further. I've also sketched something that might keep the
date roughly intact: cut 20% of merchant traffic over first, watch it for
48 hours under real load, then ramp the rest. It costs a few days of
buffer, but it means we find out if there's a ceiling problem with 80% of
volume still on the old system as a safety net."

Notice what each sentence is doing before reading on — Maya opens with
the shared goal, states the number and the comparison that makes it
concrete, pre-empts the "but the vendor said" objection by naming her own
data source, and only then offers a specific alternative instead of
leaving Marcus to solve the problem alone.

<Checkpoint question="Maya's very first sentence in the conversation is 'I want this cutover to hit the board date as much as you do.' What is that sentence actually doing, mechanically, before any data is even mentioned?">

It establishes that Maya and Marcus have the same goal before she
introduces information that could otherwise sound like an attack on his
plan. Without it, "the new system fails at 60% of peak" can land as "your
plan is bad" — a challenge to defend against. With it, the same fact
lands as "here's something standing between us and the goal we both
want" — a problem to solve together. It costs one sentence and changes
which mental mode Marcus is in for the rest of the conversation: defend,
or think.

</Checkpoint>

## What Marcus does with it

Marcus goes quiet for a moment, then asks to see the raw load-test
output. He agrees the number is concerning, but pushes back on the
timeline: "A phased cutover means telling the board the date's not solid
after I already told them it was. I need to think about this — let me
get back to you tomorrow."

The next day, Marcus tells Maya he's decided to proceed with the original
single-weekend cutover as planned, but he's asked infrastructure to
provision extra capacity headroom and have an emergency rollback script
ready as a manual break-glass option, even though the plan is still
"cut over as one event."

This is the moment upward disagreement is actually tested — not the
conversation itself, but what Maya does immediately after being heard and
then overruled.

## Disagree-and-commit: what Maya does next

Maya raised the concern clearly, with evidence, and Marcus made a
decision with that risk visible — he added mitigations, but kept the
core plan. That is meaningfully different from Marcus never having heard
the number at all. Maya's job now is **disagree-and-commit**: she thinks
the plan still carries real risk, and she executes it anyway, at full
effort, because the decision was legitimately his to make.

Concretely, over the next two weeks, that looks like:

- Helping build the emergency rollback script Marcus asked for, as
  carefully as if it were her own idea — not slow-walking it because she
  didn't get her way.
- Not repeating "I told you this might fail" in planning meetings — the
  concern was heard once; restating it without anything new just signals
  she hasn't accepted the answer.
- Staying alert for genuinely new information, because that — not
  repetition — is what would justify raising it again.

| What it is                                      | What it isn't                                             |
| ----------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| Executing the decision at full effort           | Slow-walking or quietly sabotaging what was decided       |
| Accepting the call was legitimately Marcus's    | Pretending to agree, or privately hoping it fails         |
| Staying alert for new evidence                  | Treating "I was overruled" as the end of the conversation |
| Raising again only with something genuinely new | Repeating the same concern to feel vindicated             |

<Checkpoint question="Marcus added extra capacity headroom and a rollback script but kept the single-weekend cutover. Why does that count as Maya's concern having 'worked,' even though the plan she was worried about is still, in its core shape, going ahead?">

Because the goal was never to make Marcus cancel the migration — it was
to make sure he decided with the real risk visible instead of finding out
about it live during the cutover. Before Maya's conversation, Marcus was
committing to a plan with an unknown ceiling problem and no fallback. After
it, he's committing to the same core plan, but now with headroom
provisioned and a rollback option built specifically because of the number
Maya surfaced. The plan's shape didn't change; the amount of information
and preparation behind it did. That's the actual, achievable target of
upward disagreement — informed decisions, not won arguments.

</Checkpoint>

## When escalating further would be justified — and when it wouldn't

Four days before the cutover, a second, larger load test — run by
infrastructure, using this year's Black Friday sales projections instead
of last year's actuals — shows the ceiling is even lower than Maya
thought: closer to 55% of _this_ year's projected peak, not 60% of last
year's. That is new evidence Marcus does not yet have.

This is exactly the case where raising it again is doing the job, not
being difficult — Maya messages Marcus the same day, privately, with the
same structure: shared goal, the new number, a comparison, a specific
ask ("can we push the phased option back onto the table given this new
number, even at this late stage?"). She does not say "I told you so," and
she does not need to — the new data speaks for itself, and repeating the
old argument would only dilute the new one.

Contrast that with a version where nothing new happened and Maya raises
it again anyway, four days out, simply because she's still uneasy: that
version reads as not having accepted Marcus's decision, costs trust for
the _next_ concern she raises — the one that might matter even more — and
adds pressure without adding information he doesn't already have.

| Situation four days out                 | Right move                             | Why                                                        |
| --------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| New load test shows a lower ceiling     | Raise again, once, with the new number | Marcus is deciding on information that's now out of date   |
| Nothing new, Maya is still uneasy       | Hold — support the plan as decided     | Repetition without new information reads as not letting go |
| Marcus asks Maya directly for an update | Give an honest, current read           | This isn't escalation, it's answering a question he asked  |

## Extend it: what changes if the room or the stakes are different

The conversation above worked because Maya could get 15 private minutes,
had clean data, and Marcus was willing to engage once he saw it. Before
moving on, work through what would actually change — not just get
harder — in two different versions of this same problem:

**What if Maya couldn't get Marcus alone** — say, the only chance to
raise it is in a cross-team planning review with a dozen people present,
because the cutover locks in an hour? The framing-as-inquiry and
evidence-first moves still apply, but the private-timing move isn't
available. What would Maya need to change about _how_ she frames the
question in front of a group to protect Marcus's standing in the room
while still surfacing the number — and is there a version of "can we
double-check one number together before this locks" that works publicly,
where "this plan has a serious hole in it" wouldn't?

**What if the news were worse — the system were already failing in
production**, not a pre-launch load test? The four moves (framing,
timing, evidence, follow-through) still matter, but the _timing_ trade-off
flips: waiting for a private moment costs real money and trust with every
minute that passes during a live incident, in a way it didn't during
planning. What does "disagree-and-commit" even mean once something is
actively broken — is there still a version of it, or does an active
incident change what's actually owed to the decision-maker in the
moment?

Neither question has a single correct answer here — they're worth sitting
with, because the four moves from L2 are a starting shape for this
problem, not a script that reproduces Maya's exact conversation in every
situation upward disagreement shows up in.
