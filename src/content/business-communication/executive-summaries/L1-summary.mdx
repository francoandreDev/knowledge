An engineer, Jordan, spends a week writing a careful three-page
proposal to migrate a legacy billing service before it becomes a
real outage risk. The doc opens with two paragraphs of history (how
the service was built, why it's aged badly), a paragraph of context
on the team's current workload, and finally, on page 2, the actual
ask: approval to reprioritize a quarter of roadmap capacity. A VP
skims it for maybe ninety seconds in between meetings, doesn't reach
page 2, and replies "thanks, let's discuss sometime" — which never
happens. **The doc wasn't wrong. Every sentence in it was accurate
and relevant. What made it fail anyway, and what would Jordan need
to change about the doc's _order_, not its content, to fix it?**

## Quick vocabulary

| Term                   | Plain meaning in this scenario                                          |
| ---------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Engineer               | The technical worker proposing the change                               |
| Legacy billing service | An older system that still handles customer billing                     |
| VP                     | A senior leader with limited reading time and decision authority        |
| Roadmap capacity       | The team's planned work time for the quarter                            |
| Proposal               | A document asking someone to approve a decision or action               |
| Outage risk            | The chance that a system failure affects customers or business activity |

## The shape of the problem

- A careful, accurate document can still fail completely if the
  reader never reaches the part that matters — and a busy executive
  reading in a gap between meetings decides within the first few
  lines whether to keep reading closely, skim, or set it aside.
- Most technical writing is built **narrative-first**: context, then
  reasoning, then the conclusion — which mirrors how the writer
  _discovered_ the answer, not how a time-constrained reader needs to
  _receive_ it.
- **BLUF (Bottom Line Up Front)** inverts this: state the decision
  needed, the recommendation, and the stakes in the first sentence or
  two — then let the reasoning and detail follow, for whoever has
  time to read them.

In this unit, an executive is a busy decision-maker, not necessarily a
technical reader.

## Key terms

- **BLUF (Bottom Line Up Front)** — leading with the conclusion,
  recommendation, or ask before any of the supporting context —
  originally a military-briefing convention, now standard for any
  writing aimed at a reader who might stop at any point.
- **Executive** — the person who can approve, reject, redirect, or
  escalate the ask. The word points to decision pressure more than job
  title.
- **Executive summary** — a short, self-contained opening (a
  paragraph or a few bullets) that states what's being asked and why,
  written so a reader who reads _only_ that section still knows
  what's being requested and what to decide.
- **Inverted pyramid** — a structure (borrowed from journalism)
  where each paragraph is _less_ essential than the one before it —
  the reader can stop reading at any point and still have the most
  important information.
- **The ask** — the specific decision, approval, or action the
  document wants from its reader, stated explicitly rather than left
  for the reader to infer from context.
- **Stake** — what changes if the reader says yes, says no, or waits.
- **Recommendation** — the path the writer thinks should be chosen,
  stated before the full proof.
- **Decision** — the actual choice needed from the reader.
- **Approval** — permission to proceed with a specific action,
  budget, priority, or trade-off.
- **Supporting detail** — background, reasoning, and evidence that
  justify the ask, placed _after_ the ask so it's available to a
  reader who wants it, without gating the ask behind it.

## What this unit covers

L2 works through why narrative order and executive attention are
structurally mismatched, and what specifically goes in an executive
summary's first few lines versus what belongs in supporting detail.
L3 walks Jordan's doc rewrite end to end — the original narrative-
first draft, a paragraph-by-paragraph diagnosis of what a
time-constrained reader actually does with each part, and the
BLUF rewrite that gets the same ask evaluated in the same meeting it
was raised in.
