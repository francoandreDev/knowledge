L2 laid out four moves in the abstract: map the real network, build
the coalition early, trade favors and frame around the listener's
stakes, and use a credible messenger where needed. This section runs
all four through Dev's actual twelve days.

**Before reading on: with zero allies and twelve days on the clock, which of the four tech leads should Dev approach first — and why does the order matter as much as the content of what he says?**

## Days 1–2: mapping the real network

Dev starts by writing down, honestly, who has actually changed a
decision like this before — not who's supposed to, on paper. He asks
himself three questions about each of the five people in the room:

1. **Does Farid personally have technical judgment on migration risk, or does he defer?** Answer: he defers — he's said so directly in past reviews ("I trust you all to work it out").
2. **Which of the four tech leads would Farid overrule if they objected?** Answer: none, reliably — the team has watched Farid sign whatever the four agree on, every time, for two years.
3. **Which tech lead is most affected by the outcome, and least affected?** Sana's Routing team owns the service being migrated — most affected. Aisha's Payments team touches order-routing only indirectly — least affected, and the busiest with an unrelated launch.

That gives Dev a real map: four decision-makers, one of whom (Aisha)
is unlikely to engage deeply either way, and one (Tomas) who is
already his own manager — a relationship with an existing, if thin,
trust balance to draw on.

| Person | Nominal role            | Real role in this decision                                                     | Existing relationship with Dev |
| ------ | ----------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------ |
| Farid  | Sign-off                | Defers to tech-lead consensus                                                  | Professional, distant          |
| Sana   | Tech lead — Routing     | Most affected, most informed, likely most persuadable                          | Almost none                    |
| Miguel | Tech lead — Fulfillment | Second most affected — has to freeze dependent work for a hard cutover weekend | Almost none                    |
| Tomas  | Tech lead — Platform    | Dev's own manager — some existing trust                                        | Some                           |
| Aisha  | Tech lead — Payments    | Least affected, busy with an unrelated launch                                  | None                           |

## Days 3–7: building the coalition, in order

Dev approaches people in an order chosen for leverage, not comfort:
Sana first, because she's most affected and least invested in
defending the hard-cutover plan (nobody had proposed it _to solve a
problem for her_ — it was proposed for speed). Each conversation
before the next uses what was learned or gained in the one before it.

1. **Sana (day 3).** Dev offers something real first: a review of her rollback plan for a smaller, unrelated migration she's worried about, no strings attached. A week later he raises the order-routing question, framed around her stakes: _"If something breaks during a hard cutover, you're the one paged at 2 a.m. with no fallback. An incremental migration gives your team a rollback window on your own on-call rotation."_ Sana had already been uneasy about the timeline; this names a concern she recognizes as her own. She agrees to say so if asked directly.
2. **Miguel (day 5).** Dev mentions, honestly, that Sana shares the concern — not as pressure, but as context: _"Sana's raised the same rollback-window issue for Routing. Fulfillment has to freeze all dependent changes for the cutover weekend either way — does the incremental approach help you the same way?"_ Miguel hadn't considered the freeze cost explicitly; framed around his own team's calendar, he agrees it's a real cost he'd rather avoid.
3. **Tomas (day 6).** As Dev's manager, Tomas already trusts Dev's technical judgment generally. Dev doesn't lean on that relationship as leverage — he still frames the ask around Tomas's actual stake (Platform owns the shared infrastructure both migration paths depend on, and an incremental rollout gives Platform more warning before either failure mode hits their pager too). Tomas agrees, partly on the merits and partly because two other tech leads already lean the same way.
4. **Aisha (day 9).** Dev doesn't attempt a full pitch — Aisha is busy and least affected. He sends a short, specific note: _"Sana, Miguel, and Tomas are leaning toward the incremental migration for rollback-window reasons. Payments isn't directly affected either way — flagging so it's not a surprise at the meeting."_ This isn't an ask for her support; it's making sure she isn't blindsided into defending a position she never held. She replies she has no strong preference and will defer to the others.

<Checkpoint question="By day 9, three of the four tech leads lean toward the incremental migration, and Dev has never spoken to VP Farid about it directly. Using the real-vs-nominal map from L2, is Dev behind schedule for not having briefed Farid yet — or right on track?">

Right on track. Farid is the nominal decision-maker who defers to
tech-lead consensus — the real decision has effectively already moved
once three of the four tech leads independently reach the same
position. Briefing Farid before that consensus existed would have
been arguing to the wrong map at the wrong time; briefing him now, once
there's a real recommendation to describe, is the efficient use of
that conversation, not a delay.

</Checkpoint>

## Days 10–11: the credible messenger, for the part Dev can't carry himself

One open question remains: is the risk itself — outages during peak
shipping season — real enough to worry about, or is Dev overstating
it? Dev isn't on-call for order-routing and has never run a cutover
incident personally; his case here is thinner than his case for the
rollback-window framing, which he could argue directly. This is
exactly the gap a credible messenger closes. Dev asks Priya, the SRE
lead who has run three prior cutover incidents, to send a short,
factual note to Farid and the tech leads: incident timestamps, what
broke, how long the previous hard cutovers took to recover. Priya's
standing on this specific question is unquestioned — nobody asks her
"why are you worried about this?" the way they might ask Dev. Her
email isn't advocacy; it's three data points that speak for
themselves, and it lands with weight Dev's own version of the same
words wouldn't have carried.

## Day 12: the meeting

By the meeting, three tech leads already lean incremental, Aisha has
no objection, and Priya's incident data is already in everyone's
inbox. The meeting doesn't need to be won from a standing start — it
needs to confirm what several people already independently believe.
Farid asks the four tech leads for their recommendation; three say
incremental, citing the rollback window and freeze-cost reasons in
their own words; Aisha defers. Farid signs the incremental migration.
Dev speaks once, briefly, to answer a scheduling question — not to
make the case, because the case was already made, days earlier, by
five different conversations that each did one small piece of the
work.

## Extend it: what if the room had been split, not leaning?

**Suppose Aisha, instead of having no strong preference, actively favored the hard cutover for an unrelated reason — say, her Payments team wants the whole migration finished before their own unrelated launch, and a month of parallel systems threatens that timeline. Does Dev's plan still work with only 2 of 4 tech leads leaning his way instead of 3?**

Not automatically — 2 of 4 is not yet the majority the interactive
model treats as a threshold. Dev's options change, not disappear: he
could look for a way to solve Aisha's actual problem (could the
incremental migration be sequenced to protect her launch date rather
than delay it?), which reframes her opposition as a solvable
constraint rather than a fixed veto — or he could accept a genuinely
split room and let the meeting surface the disagreement honestly,
rather than forcing an artificial consensus. **What changes about the
right move if Aisha's objection were about risk tolerance rather than
a schedule conflict — is a scheduling fix still the right lever, or
does that call for a different one of the four moves entirely?**

A risk-tolerance objection wouldn't be fixed by resequencing anything —
it would call for the same tool used on Farid's uncertainty: a
credible messenger. If Aisha's actual worry were "is this migration
approach even safe," Priya's incident data — not a scheduling
compromise — is the lever that addresses it, because it targets the
real objection instead of a nearby one that happens to be easier to
solve.

## Failure modes

| Failure mode                                                     | What it gets wrong                                                                                                                     |
| ---------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Pitching the nominal decision-maker and stopping there           | Farid defers to the tech leads; convincing him alone doesn't move the real decision                                                    |
| Raising the concern for the first time in the meeting            | No time for anyone to genuinely reconsider — positions default to whatever they walked in with                                         |
| Approaching every tech lead with the identical pitch             | Ignores that each person has different real stakes; a rollback-window framing means little to Aisha                                    |
| Treating a past favor as a blank check for any future ask        | The size of an ask still has to roughly match what's been deposited — a small favor doesn't buy public support on a contested decision |
| Arguing the risk case personally when standing is the actual gap | No amount of restating the argument fixes a credibility gap; a credible messenger fixes it, more argument doesn't                      |
| Treating 2 of 4 tech leads as "close enough" to a majority       | The threshold effect is real — falling short of a majority of real decision-makers leaves the outcome genuinely open                   |

## The same four moves, outside this one decision

None of this is specific to migrations. The same four moves apply
any time someone needs a decision to go a certain way without owning
it: map who really decides versus who's nominally listed; build
agreement in private conversations before the moment that counts, in
an order chosen for leverage; trade something genuinely useful instead
of asking for something free, sized to match the ask; and frame the
request around what the other person already has a reason to want,
not around your own worry. Where personal standing runs out on a
specific sub-question, hand that one piece — not the whole case — to
someone whose standing on exactly that question isn't in doubt.
