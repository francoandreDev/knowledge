Marcus, a staff engineer at an online retailer, spends fifteen
minutes in a leadership sync explaining that the checkout database's
primary connection pool is running at 62% utilization under normal
load, that a recent query-plan regression is adding latency under
concurrent writes, and that without intervention they risk connection
exhaustion under peak load. Elena, the COO, listens, asks one
clarifying question about whether "this is like the outage from last
year," and when Marcus says "not exactly, it's more of a capacity
ceiling issue," she says "okay, sounds important — keep an eye on it
and loop in the team if it gets worse," and moves to the next agenda
item. Nothing gets funded, staffed, or scheduled. **Marcus's technical
facts were accurate. What did the explanation fail to give Elena that
she needed in order to actually make a decision?**

## Quick vocabulary

| Term                        | Plain meaning in this scenario                                                                     |
| --------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Technical risk              | A fact about a system's behavior under some condition (load, an attacker, time) — engineer-native  |
| Business risk               | What that technical fact could cost the business — revenue, customers, legal exposure, trust       |
| Probability                 | How likely the bad outcome is, and how that likelihood is changing over time                       |
| Impact                      | What it costs if the bad outcome happens — in dollars, customers, or exposure, not severity jargon |
| Exposure window / "by when" | The concrete date or milestone by which the risk becomes real if nothing changes                   |
| Option                      | A concrete path forward with its own cost and how much risk it removes — not a single verdict      |

## The shape of the problem

- A technically accurate explanation can still produce no decision at
  all — Elena didn't reject Marcus's request, she just never received
  one she could act on. "Keep an eye on it" is what a business leader
  says when they've heard a fact but not a decision to make.
- Engineers default to describing risk the way they experience it:
  mechanism first (connection pools, query plans, percentages of a
  limit). A non-technical leader needs to know what it costs and by
  when, not how it works internally.
- Translating technical risk to business risk means re-expressing the
  same underlying fact in terms of **probability** (how likely, and
  by when), **impact** (what it costs if it happens), and **options**
  (what could be done about it, and what each costs) — without either
  inflating the fact into a false emergency or downplaying it into
  something safely ignorable.

In this unit, "the business leader" is someone who allocates budget,
staffing, or priority but doesn't necessarily understand the
technical mechanism — the same audience gap as `executive-summaries`,
but this unit is specifically about representing **risk**, which adds
its own translation problem: severity and urgency are technical
judgments that don't automatically map onto business ones.

## Key terms

- **Technical risk** — a fact about how a system behaves under some
  condition, described in engineering vocabulary (a percentage, a
  known vulnerability, a growth curve).
- **Business risk** — the same underlying fact re-expressed in terms
  of what the business stands to lose: revenue, customers, legal
  standing, reputation, or future capacity to deliver.
- **Probability** — not a single yes/no, but how likely the risk is
  to materialize, and importantly, how that likelihood changes as
  time passes without action.
- **Impact** — the concrete cost if the risk materializes, ideally in
  units the listener already tracks (dollars, customers, hours of
  downtime) rather than technical severity scales.
- **Exposure window** — the specific point at which "probably fine
  for now" becomes "actively happening" — a date, a milestone, or an
  event, not a vague sense of urgency.
- **Catastrophizing** — describing a risk as more certain or more
  severe than the evidence supports, which trains listeners to
  discount the next warning too.
- **Minimizing** — describing a risk as less certain or less severe
  than the evidence supports, often to avoid an uncomfortable
  conversation, which leaves the listener under-informed for the
  decision they're actually making.
- **Option** — one concrete path forward, with its own cost, timeline,
  and how much of the risk it actually removes — offered alongside at
  least one alternative, not delivered as a single technical verdict.

## What this unit covers

L2 works through the mechanics of translating a technical fact into
probability, impact, and a concrete "if nothing changes, by when"
timeline, plus how to avoid both catastrophizing and minimizing. L3
walks Marcus's checkout-database risk end to end — the original
jargon-heavy explanation, a diagnosis of exactly what Elena couldn't
act on, and the business-framed rewrite that gets her to a real
decision between concrete options.
