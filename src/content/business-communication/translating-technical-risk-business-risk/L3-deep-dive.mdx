## The original explanation, and what Elena actually does with it

**Before rewriting anything — what does Elena hear, statement by
statement, and what can she actually do with each one?** Reading
Marcus's original explanation the way Elena would, as a business
leader trying to decide whether this needs budget or staffing now,
makes the failure concrete instead of abstract.

> **Statement 1.** "Our checkout database's primary connection pool
> is running at 62% utilization under normal load."
>
> **What Elena does with this:** Registers it as a number without a
> reference point — is 62% fine or alarming? Nothing in the sentence
> says, so it reads as a status update, not a decision point.

> **Statement 2.** "We've also got a query-plan regression adding
> latency under concurrent writes, which is compounding the pool
> pressure."
>
> **What Elena does with this:** Doesn't have the technical
> background to evaluate "query-plan regression" or "pool pressure,"
> and nothing in the sentence tells her she needs to — it sounds like
> engineering detail, the kind of thing she'd expect the engineering
> team to just handle.

> **Statement 3.** "Without intervention, we risk connection
> exhaustion under peak load."
>
> **What Elena does with this:** "Risk" and "could" register as
> hedged and open-ended — no date, no size of impact, no comparison
> to anything she already tracks. This is the point she asks whether
> it's "like the outage from last year," trying to translate the
> statement into business terms herself.

> **Statement 4 (Marcus's actual reply).** "Not exactly, it's more of
> a capacity ceiling issue."
>
> **What Elena hears:** More technical framing, in response to a
> question that was itself an attempt to get a business-risk
> comparison. The conversation never produces a cost, a date, or a
> choice — so "keep an eye on it" is the only reasonable response to
> what's actually been said.

**Every statement was true. None of them, individually or together,
told Elena what this costs or by when she needs to decide anything.**

<Checkpoint question="Elena's question — 'is this like the outage from last year?' — was itself a request for a business-risk translation. What did Marcus's answer fail to give her, and why did the conversation stall there?">

Elena was asking Marcus to compare this risk to a business outcome
she already understood — last year's outage, with its own known cost
and disruption. That question was Marcus's opening to answer in
those terms: yes/no, and here's what makes this one bigger or
smaller in cost and likelihood. Instead, "it's more of a capacity
ceiling issue" stayed in technical framing, handing the translation
work back to Elena — who doesn't have the technical background to do
it herself. The conversation stalls exactly there because that was
the last moment a translation could have happened before the meeting
moved on.

</Checkpoint>

## The rewrite: the same facts, translated

**What does the exact same set of technical facts look like once
translated into probability, impact, a date, and options?** Nothing
about the underlying system changed — only how the facts are framed:

> **Opening — the ask and the timeline:** "I need a decision this
> week on checkout database capacity. At current traffic growth
> (about 8%/month), we project the primary database crosses its
> practical capacity limit around week 11 — mid-October. At
> Black-Friday-level traffic, which historically runs about 3x normal
> load, we're already over that limit **today**, not in the future."
>
> **Impact, in terms Elena already tracks:** "If checkout starts
> failing under load, based on last year's partial outage, we lose
> roughly $18,000 in sales per minute of downtime during peak hours,
> plus the kind of visible failure during a high-traffic sale event
> that tends to show up in customer complaints and press, not just
> that day's revenue."
>
> **Options, not a verdict:**

| Option                                         | Cost                | What it buys                                                                                         |
| ---------------------------------------------- | ------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Quick mitigation (connection pooling config)   | ~1 engineer-week    | Buys roughly 2 extra months of runway; doesn't fix Black Friday exposure                             |
| Add a read replica, offload read-heavy queries | ~3–4 engineer-weeks | Buys 6–12 months of runway; likely enough to clear this Black Friday safely                          |
| Re-architect for horizontal scaling (sharding) | ~1 quarter          | Durable fix, needed eventually regardless of the other two; too slow to help this Black Friday alone |

> **Recommendation, stated after the options, not instead of them:**
> "I'd recommend the read replica now, funded this week, with the
> sharding work queued for next quarter regardless of what we decide
> today — the quick mitigation alone doesn't cover Black Friday, and
> the full re-architecture won't finish in time to help this year
> either."

**If Elena hears only the opening two paragraphs and has to leave for
another meeting, what does she already know?** That a decision is
needed this week, what it costs if she waits, and that Black Friday
is already inside the danger zone — enough to at least approve
funding the read replica before the meeting ends, even without
reaching the full options table.

<Checkpoint question="The rewrite states the Black Friday exposure ('we're already over that limit today') before the 11-week projection for normal traffic. Why put the more alarming fact first rather than building up to it?">

Because Elena's actual decision window is bounded by Black Friday,
not by the 11-week normal-traffic projection — the more distant
number is true but less decision-relevant given the calendar she's
actually planning against. Leading with the nearer, more concrete
exposure (today, under peak-level load) gives her the fact she needs
most to decide urgency correctly; the 11-week figure adds useful
context about the underlying trend but isn't what should determine
whether this gets funded this week. This mirrors the executive-
summaries principle of ordering by what a stopped-here reader needs
most, applied to which fact carries the most decision weight, not
just which one comes first chronologically.

</Checkpoint>

## Why this isn't just "add a dollar figure"

**Could Marcus have fixed the original explanation by keeping
everything else the same and just tacking "this could cost us money"
onto the end?** No — a vague cost claim with no date and no options
still leaves Elena with nothing to compare against her calendar or to
choose between. The fix wasn't adding one dollar amount; it was
restructuring the entire explanation around the three things a
business decision actually needs: a specific "by when," an impact in
units Elena already tracks, and at least two costed paths forward.
Dropping in a dollar figure without the date or the options is
still, functionally, a technical statement with a number attached —
not a translation.

## What generalizes and what doesn't

The translation pattern — probability with a "by when," impact in
business units, calibrated (not catastrophized or minimized), options
instead of a verdict — generalizes to almost any technical risk
raised to a non-technical decision-maker: a security vulnerability, a
compliance gap, a vendor dependency risk. What's specific to Marcus's
case: capacity risk projects forward using a growth rate and a known
event (Black Friday) as the natural "by when" anchor. A different
kind of technical risk needs a different anchor for its timeline — a
security vulnerability's "by when" usually isn't a growth curve, it's
tied to how quickly a known flaw becomes easier to exploit as it gets
more publicly documented. **Try extending it yourself:** if Marcus
were instead reporting a newly disclosed vulnerability in a
third-party authentication library the checkout service depends on —
not a capacity problem, but an actively-exploitable flaw with no
patch yet available — would "at current growth, we cross capacity
around week 11" still be the right kind of "by when" claim, or does
this different technical risk need a different kind of timeline
anchor and a different kind of impact statement (say, exposure to
legal/compliance risk from a data breach rather than lost checkout
revenue)?

A vulnerability's timeline usually isn't derived from a growth
curve — it's driven by how public the flaw is (a CVE became public
today, so time-to-exploit is shrinking, not a projected trend line)
and whether a vendor patch exists yet. The impact side would likely
shift too: instead of "lost sales during downtime," the dominant cost
might be regulatory exposure or breach-notification costs if customer
data were involved — a different business unit than lost revenue, but
still expressed as a probability, an impact in dollars/legal terms,
a "by when," and options, following the same underlying pattern.

## Failure modes

| Failure mode                                                                             | What it gets wrong                                                                                                                                              |
| ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Believing accurate technical detail is enough to produce a decision                      | Accuracy doesn't translate into decidability — a leader needs cost and a date, not just correct facts about the mechanism                                       |
| Answering a business-comparison question ("is this like X?") with more technical framing | Hands the translation work back to a listener who can't do it — the question was the opening to translate, not a request for more mechanism                     |
| Adding a dollar figure without a date or options                                         | A costed-but-undated, verdict-only statement is still a technical statement with a number attached, not a full translation                                      |
| Leading with the more distant, less decision-relevant number                             | Should order by what the listener's actual decision window needs first, not by chronological or technical build-up                                              |
| Assuming every technical risk shares the same "by when" anchor as this one               | Capacity risk projects from a growth curve; other risks (a disclosed vulnerability, a compliance gap) need a timeline anchored to their own real driver instead |
