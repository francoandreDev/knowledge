GreenCart's checkout handled a flash sale without a single error. Every order
was processed correctly. Then the cloud invoice arrived: orders had grown 6x,
but the bill had grown 16.5x. **The system was correct the whole time, so why
did finance treat this as an incident?**

| Metric         | Before  | Sale month | Multiplier |
| -------------- | ------- | ---------- | ---------- |
| Orders/month   | 1M      | 6M         | 6x         |
| Cloud bill     | $40,000 | $660,000   | 16.5x      |
| Cost per order | $0.04   | $0.11      | 2.75x      |

All figures in this unit are illustrative assumptions, not measurements from a
real company.

</Scenario>

Because correct was never the only requirement. A system that answers every
request right but costs more per order as it grows does not scale. It just
fails later, on a different dashboard.

## Is cost a design concern or a finance concern?

**If nothing broke, who owns the problem?** The architecture does. Cost is a
**non-functional requirement**, in the same family as latency and availability:
a design is judged on whether it does the job _and_ whether it can keep doing it
at a price the business can sustain. The only difference is the clock. An outage
shows up on a monitor in seconds; a cost failure shows up on a monthly invoice.

## Which number tells you the design is failing?

**Total spend doubled. Is that bad?** Not by itself. If the business grew 6x
and spend grew 2x, that is a healthy system. The signal is **cost per unit of
value**: cost per order, per active user, per GB processed.

| Total spend | Volume | Cost per unit | Reading                                     |
| ----------- | ------ | ------------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| Up 2x       | Up 6x  | Falling       | Healthy: growth made each unit cheaper      |
| Up 16.5x    | Up 6x  | Rising        | Warning: the design does not scale in money |
| Flat        | Flat   | Flat          | Nothing to learn yet                        |

GreenCart sits on the second row. Rising cost per unit while volume grows means
the architecture scales technically but not economically.

## Where does the money actually go, and what can you pull?

**"The bill went up" is not actionable. What would make it actionable?**
Splitting it. Part of a bill is **fixed** (capacity you pay for even at 3 a.m.
with no traffic) and part is **variable** (metered compute, storage, data
transfer, per-call third-party fees). Which lever helps depends on which part is
out of control.

```mermaid
flowchart LR
    S["Total cloud spend"] --> F["Fixed cost\n(baseline capacity you pay for\neven when idle)"]
    S --> V["Variable cost\n(metered compute, storage,\ndata transfer, per-call fees)"]
    F --> D1["Levers: committed pricing,\nright-sizing the baseline"]
    V --> D2["Levers: caching, batching,\nscaling back down, spot for\nretry-safe work"]
```

Four pricing models cover most choices, and the right one depends on how
predictable and how interruptible the load is:

| Model                       | You commit to                    | Best for                             |
| --------------------------- | -------------------------------- | ------------------------------------ |
| On-demand                   | Nothing                          | Unpredictable or early-stage load    |
| Reserved / committed-use    | Baseline capacity for a term     | Steady, predictable load             |
| Spot / preemptible          | Nothing, but it can be reclaimed | Interruptible, retry-safe batch work |
| Serverless (pay per invoke) | Nothing                          | Spiky load with a near-zero baseline |

Two more things are easy to miss on an architecture diagram but never on the
invoice: data transfer between regions, and logging or monitoring ingestion.
And none of it is fixable without **cost attribution** by service and feature:
"checkout's database writes tripled" is a task, "the bill went up" is a mood.
