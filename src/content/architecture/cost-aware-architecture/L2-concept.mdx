## What shape should a healthy cost curve have?

**If a service has a fixed monthly cost and a constant cost per order, what
happens to cost per order as volume grows?** Write the model as:

```
total(orders)        = fixed + variablePerOrder * orders
costPerOrder(orders) = fixed / orders + variablePerOrder
```

The `fixed / orders` term shrinks toward zero as orders grow, so cost per order
falls toward `variablePerOrder` from above. That is economies of scale, and it
is the shape a healthy system should have. Using assumed numbers of a $20,000
fixed cost and $0.015 per order, the curve falls with volume:

| Orders/month | Fixed share ($20,000 / orders) | Variable | Cost per order |
| ------------ | ------------------------------ | -------- | -------------- |
| 1M           | $0.0200                        | $0.015   | $0.0350        |
| 2M           | $0.0100                        | $0.015   | $0.0250        |
| 4M           | $0.0050                        | $0.015   | $0.0200        |
| 6M           | $0.0033                        | $0.015   | $0.0183        |

<Checkpoint question="If cost per order should fall as volume grows, why did GreenCart's rise from $0.04 to $0.11?">

Because `variablePerOrder` was not constant. A database write on every request,
with no cache, means each extra order adds load to a database that gets
proportionally more expensive as concurrent load rises: more connections, more
contention, more capacity bought at on-demand rates. The formula's variable term
is only a flat constant when the resource scales cheaply and linearly. When it
does not, the variable term itself grows, and that outweighs the shrinking fixed
share. The system moves from economies of scale to diseconomies of scale.

</Checkpoint>

## Why do A and B point in opposite directions?

**What did Service B do that Service A did not?** Service A pays for a database
write on every request and only ever adds on-demand capacity: its per-order
variable cost climbs with volume, and its fixed cost buys no discount. Service
B serves the read-heavy majority of checkout traffic (inventory and price
lookups) from a cache, batches writes, and covers its predictable baseline with
reserved capacity. Its variable cost stays near flat, and its fixed cost is
spread over more orders. The chart plots assumed values, with B computed from
the formula above:

```mermaid
    line "Service B (cache + reserved baseline)" [0.035, 0.025, 0.0217, 0.020, 0.0183]
```

The chart is not two opinions. It is one formula with different values plugged
in, and with different answers to whether the variable term stays constant.
Architecture chose the curve before a single order shipped.

## How do you pick a pricing model without picking only one?

**Reserved is cheaper per hour. Spot is cheaper still. Why not use the cheapest
for everything?** Because each discount is bought with a constraint, and the
right trade depends on two properties of the workload: is it steady, and can it
be interrupted?

```mermaid
flowchart TD
    Q1{"Is load steady\nand predictable?"}
    Q1 -->|Yes| R["Reserved / committed capacity\nfor that baseline"]
    Q1 -->|"No, it is spiky"| Q2{"Is the work interruptible\nand safe to retry?"}
    Q2 -->|Yes| SP["Spot / preemptible\nfor that portion"]
    Q2 -->|No| Q3{"Is baseline traffic\nnear zero much of the time?"}
    Q3 -->|Yes| SL["Serverless,\npay per invocation"]
    Q3 -->|No| OD["On-demand with autoscaling;\nreserve only the true floor"]
```

Most real systems are a **blend**: a reserved baseline sized to the traffic
floor, autoscaled on-demand for the middle, and spot or serverless for the
bursty or retry-safe edges. GreenCart's failure was not choosing on-demand. It
was choosing _only_ on-demand for a load with a very predictable daily floor.

<Checkpoint question="GreenCart's traffic has a predictable daily floor plus occasional unpredictable sale spikes. One pricing model for everything, or a split?">

A split. The daily floor is exactly what committed capacity is for: it exists
every day, so committing earns a discount with no risk of paying for unused
capacity. The spikes are the opposite case: committing in advance for a spike
that may not come, or may be 3x bigger than planned, either wastes money or
fails to scale. That part stays on-demand with autoscaling, so it grows with
what shows up and shrinks afterward.

</Checkpoint>
