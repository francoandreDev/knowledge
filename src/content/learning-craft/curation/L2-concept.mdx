## Why do extra hours of raw reading buy so little?

**If Elena doubled her hours, would she know twice as much?** No, and the
reason is structural. Suppose (an assumed model, not a measurement) the field
produces about 2 genuinely useful items a week no matter how much noise
surrounds them, and each extra hour finds a smaller share of what is left.
The result is a curve that rises fast and then goes flat:

```mermaid
xychart-beta
    title "Modeled useful items surfaced per week vs. raw-reading hours"
    x-axis ["0 hrs", "1 hr", "2 hrs", "3 hrs", "5 hrs", "10 hrs", "15 hrs"]
    y-axis "Useful items" 0 --> 2
    line [0, 0.52, 0.9, 1.19, 1.55, 1.9, 1.98]
```

The first hour surfaces about 0.52 of the 2 items. Elena's hours 2 to 10 add
about 1.38 more, so at 10 hours she does surface about 95% of what is there
(1.90 of 2). What she pays is ten times the time, and a pile of fizzles mixed
in that she cannot sort. Her hours 11 to 15 would add just 0.08. The demo below lets
you drag the hours and watch the flattening. Attention is a small, fixed
budget while volume is effectively unbounded, so the lever that matters is
not more reading. It is filtering before reading starts.

## What does a curation system look like?

Instead of reading the raw stream, put a trusted filter between the flood and
you. It reads the volume and keeps only what survived scrutiny, and you
review its output on a schedule.

```mermaid
flowchart LR
    A["Raw stream<br/>everything published"] --> B{"Trusted filters<br/>curators, digests"}
    B -- "most items dropped" --> X["Never reaches you"]
    B -- "survives scrutiny" --> C["Batched review<br/>once on a fixed schedule"]
    C --> D["Actually read<br/>or acted on"]
```

The funnel does two jobs at once. It shrinks volume, so most of what is
published never needs to reach you. And it adds delay, so a claim has to
survive a little scrutiny before it is repeated. Elena skipped both, so a
loud-but-unproven claim reached her with the same weight as one that had
been checked.

<Checkpoint question="Diego's curators read the same raw flood Elena does. Isn't he just seeing the same items later? What does the funnel change besides timing?">

It changes which items arrive, not just when. The middle step drops most of
the stream, so Diego never sees the four claims that fizzled; his curators did
not repeat them. The delay is what makes dropping possible, since a fizzle
cannot be told from a real finding the moment it is published. But the drop,
not the delay on its own, is the value.

## How do you tell signal from hype before proof exists?

Even a good filter lets some hype through. Three heuristics help sort a claim
in the moment:

| Heuristic              | What it says                                                                                     | Why it works                                                                                                                 |
| ---------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Recency check          | New is not automatically important; ask whether it is more significant or only more recent       | There is always a stream of new items, so rewarding newness means chasing whatever arrived today                             |
| Regression to the mean | The loudest early claims tend to be overstated and moderate later                                | Early reports often come from whoever framed a result most dramatically; time and repeat evidence pull claims toward reality |
| Track-record trust     | Weight a source by whether its past "this matters" calls held up, not by how confident it sounds | Confidence is free to fake, while a record of being right is expensive to fake                                               |

These are odds, not guarantees. A claim that is both brand new and from a
source with no record is the profile of Elena's four fizzles, and it is what a
track-record filter drops routinely.

<Checkpoint question="A brand-new source posts a dramatic claim. By the recency and track-record heuristics, should it be dismissed as hype?">

Down-weighted, not dismissed. Most loud, brand-new, no-record claims are
overstated, but some genuine early findings do come from first-time sources.
The practical move is not to ignore it but to keep it out of the fast lane:
let it sit a week and see whether it survives, is picked up by a source with a
record, or is quietly walked back. The heuristic buys time before belief, not
a permanent verdict.

</Checkpoint>
