Ravi and Ines each have an 8-month-old just starting solid foods. The same
evening, each asks the same AI assistant a specific question: "Is a small
spoonful of honey in oatmeal safe for an 8-month-old trying it for the
first time?" The AI answers fluently and confidently for both of them —
yes, in small amounts, once a baby is past 6 months and already eating
solids, honey is fine. It even adds a reassuring line about honey being a
gentle natural sweetener. Ravi reads it, it sounds informed and specific,
and stirs a spoonful into his daughter's oatmeal that night. Ines reads
the identical answer — but before acting on anything the AI tells her
about her daughter's safety, she has a habit: check the one specific
claim that matters against a source she actually trusts. Thirty seconds
later, her pediatrician's own patient page says plainly: honey should
never be given to a baby under 12 months, because of a real risk of
infant botulism. She skips the honey and calls the office to ask what to
use instead. Ravi finds out the same fact two days later, from a worried
comment on a parenting forum, and spends a bad afternoon on the phone
with a nurse line double-checking his daughter is fine.

**Same question. Same AI. The exact same wrong answer, word for word. What
did Ines do, in the thirty seconds between reading the answer and acting
on it, that Ravi didn't?**

She didn't ask a better question, and she isn't more of an expert on
infant nutrition than Ravi is. She just never lets a claim like this one
move from "the AI said so" to "something I now believe and act on"
without one specific check first.

## The shape of the problem

- A large language model is built to produce the most _plausible-sounding
  next words_ given everything it's seen — not to look something up and
  confirm it's true before answering. Most of the time those two things
  line up. Sometimes they don't, and the answer is still delivered with
  the exact same fluent, confident tone either way.
- This is a genuinely new failure mode. A badly-sourced blog post usually
  signals its own shakiness — no author, no citations, a sketchy layout.
  An AI's wrong answer carries none of those tells. It's grammatically
  perfect, specific, personalized to your exact question, and reads _more_
  authoritative than a shaky article — which is precisely what makes it
  dangerous.
- **Fluency is not evidence of correctness.** An answer can be completely
  wrong and still be smooth, specific, and delivered without a flicker of
  hesitation. Confidence is a property of how the text was generated, not
  a signal that it was checked against reality.
- Feeling like you understand something after an AI explains it can be
  the exact same illusion of competence as feeling like you understood
  something after reading it once — except now there's an added risk: the
  thing you "understood" might not even be true.

## Two very different ways to use an AI to learn

- **Outsourcing understanding** — asking the AI once, accepting the
  answer at face value, and treating the exchange as done. This trades
  the effortful part of learning (working something out, checking it,
  making it yours) for a fast answer that feels like understanding but
  is really just having been told something.
- **AI as a learning accelerant** — using the AI to get oriented fast,
  then doing the (much smaller) remaining work of verifying the specific
  claims that matter before they become part of what you believe. The
  speed gain is real and worth keeping; the missing step is what needs
  adding back in.
- The difference isn't whether you use AI. It's whether a claim you got
  from it ever gets checked against something independent before it
  changes what you do.

## What a verification habit actually looks like

- Ask the AI to show its reasoning or name a source for a specific claim
  — then actually open that source, rather than treating "it cited
  something" as proof on its own.
- For a claim that's surprising, or that a real decision will rest on,
  check it against one independent source before relying on it — a
  claim you'd have bet against, or one where being wrong is expensive,
  deserves a check even if most casual questions don't.
- Try applying the explanation somewhere slightly new. If it only ever
  gets nodded along to and never tested against a fresh case, a wrong
  or incomplete explanation can sit unnoticed for a long time.
- Scale the scrutiny to the stakes: a fun trivia fact barely needs
  checking; a claim that a baby's safety, a diagnosis, or a large
  purchase will be based on always does.

## Key terms

- **Hallucination / confident-wrongness** — an AI answer that is fluent,
  specific, and stated with full confidence, while being factually wrong
  or subtly incomplete.
- **Load-bearing claim** — a claim a real decision or later reasoning
  will actually rest on, as opposed to a passing detail that changes
  nothing if it's slightly off.
- **Verification habit** — a small, repeatable check performed on a
  specific claim, before it's treated as true, rather than a one-time
  general skepticism applied inconsistently.
- **Outsourcing understanding** — using an AI's answer as a replacement
  for doing the understanding yourself, rather than as a tool that still
  requires you to build and check that understanding.
