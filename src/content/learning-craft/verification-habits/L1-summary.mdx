Priya and Tom split an $84 dinner bill four ways, and each types it into a
phone calculator. Priya types 84 / 4 and sees 21. Tom's thumb slips and he types
840 / 4. The screen shows 210, in the same clean digits and the same calm font
as Priya's 21.

Priya glances at Tom's screen and says: "84 is about 80, and 80 split four ways
is about 20. Two hundred ten can't be right." Tom retypes it and gets 21. (The
$84 bill is a made-up teaching number.)

**The calculator did its arithmetic perfectly both times, and nothing on the
screen separated the right number from the wrong one. What did Priya do, in five
seconds, that made her number trustworthy and Tom's not?**

</Scenario>

Priya did not distrust the calculator, and she did not redo the work by hand.
She ran one rough check before she acted on the number. AI assistants need the
same habit, with one extra twist: the wrong part can come from inside the tool,
not from your thumb.

## Why does a smooth answer feel like a checked one?

**If an AI answer is fluent, specific and confident, what does that tell you
about whether it is true?** Almost nothing, and that is the whole problem.

- An AI model is built to produce the most plausible-sounding next words, not to
  look a claim up before it answers. Most of the time plausible and true line
  up. Sometimes they do not, and the answer arrives in the same tone either way.
- A shaky blog post usually shows its shakiness: no author, no sources, a messy
  page. A wrong AI answer has none of those tells.
- **Fluency, confidence and detail are properties of how the text reads, not
  evidence that anyone checked it.**

| What you notice about the answer | Does it tell you the claim is true? |
| -------------------------------- | ----------------------------------- |
| It is grammatically perfect      | No                                  |
| It is specific and detailed      | No                                  |
| It sounds sure of itself         | No                                  |
| It names a source                | Not until you open the source       |
| You checked it independently     | Yes, this is the only row that does |

## Which claims are worth checking?

**You cannot check every sentence an AI writes, so which ones earn the five
seconds?** The ones something real rests on.

- A **load-bearing claim** is one a decision, a grade, a purchase or someone's
  safety will lean on. If it is wrong, something breaks.
- A passing detail that changes nothing if it is off is not worth a check.
- A claim that surprises you, or that you would have bet against, also earns a
  check, because a surprising claim is where an error is most likely to hide.

| The stakes if the claim is wrong            | How much checking it earns                        |
| ------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- |
| Nothing rides on it (idle trivia)           | Usually none                                      |
| A small, cheap-to-undo mistake              | A quick sanity check                              |
| Money, a grade, a health or safety decision | One independent source before acting              |
| A decision hard or impossible to undo       | More than one independent source, and a slow-down |

## Two ways to use an AI to learn

- **Outsourcing understanding:** ask once, accept the answer, treat the matter as
  closed. It feels like learning, but you were only told something.
- **AI as an accelerant:** use the AI to get oriented fast, then spend the small
  extra effort to check the claims that matter before they become what you
  believe. You keep the speed and you keep your judgment in use.

The difference is not whether you use AI. It is whether a claim you got from it
ever gets checked against something independent before it changes what you do.
L2 turns this into a loop you can run.

## Key terms

- **Confident wrongness:** a fluent, specific, confident answer that is wrong or
  subtly incomplete.
- **Load-bearing claim:** a claim a real decision rests on.
- **Verification habit:** a small, repeatable check on a specific claim before
  you treat it as true.
- **Outsourcing understanding:** letting the answer replace the work of
  understanding and checking.
