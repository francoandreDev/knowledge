Ben and Rosa have both guessed whether it'll rain before leaving the house,
every morning, for twenty years — roughly the same number of mornings,
the same town, the same shifting sky. Both have gotten a "feel" for it.
When Ben feels sure — the kind of sure he'd call "90% confident" — it
only actually rains about 58% of the time. When Rosa says she's "90%
confident," it rains about 89% of the time — almost exactly as often as
she said it would, across hundreds of mornings she's said it.
**Same twenty years of looking at the same sky. Why is one of them
still consistently overconfident, and the other one nearly always
right about how sure to be?**

The difference isn't that Rosa is smarter, or has read more about
weather. It's that Rosa does one small thing every single morning that
Ben never does: she writes down her percentage _before_ she knows the
answer, and checks it against reality that evening. Ben just guesses,
walks outside, and moves on — win or lose, he never actually finds
out whether "90% sure" was a fair thing to have said.

## The shape of the problem

- **Calibration is about your confidence, not your facts.** It's not "do you know the right answer" — it's "when you say you're 80% sure, are you right about 80% of the time across many such calls?" You can be knowledgeable and badly calibrated, or modestly informed and well-calibrated.
- **There are two distinct ways to be miscalibrated**, and they're opposites:
  - **Overconfident** — your stated confidence is higher than your actual hit rate (Ben: says 90%, right 58% of the time).
  - **Underconfident** — your stated confidence is lower than your actual hit rate (someone who's right 90% of the time but only ever says "maybe, 60%").
- **Well-calibrated ≠ always right.** A well-calibrated forecaster who says "80% sure" is _supposed_ to be wrong about 1 time in 5 — that's what 80% means. The goal is matching your stated confidence to your real hit rate, not driving your hit rate to 100%.
- **Experience alone doesn't fix this.** Ben has the same twenty years Rosa has. Repetition without ever checking a prediction against what actually happened teaches almost nothing about how confident you _should_ be.
- **The fix is a small, repeatable loop:** state a confidence level before you know the answer → observe what actually happens → compare the two → adjust. This is what Rosa does every morning and Ben never does.
- **This is exactly what good technical judgment is made of** — estimating how long a task will really take, how risky a change really is, how likely a debugging hypothesis is: all of these are calibration problems in disguise, and they respond to the same predict-and-check loop.
