Ben and Rosa have each guessed whether it will rain before leaving the house
for twenty years, in the same town under the same sky. Both have a "feel" for
it. On the 200 mornings when Ben felt sure enough to say "90% confident," it
rained on 116 of them, which is 58%. On the 200 mornings when Rosa said "90%
confident," it rained on 178, which is 89%.

**Same sky and same years. Why is one of them still far too sure of himself,
while the other is almost exactly as sure as she should be?**

The gap is not intelligence or weather knowledge. Rosa writes her percentage
down _before_ she knows the answer and checks it that evening. Ben guesses,
walks outside, and moves on, so he never finds out whether "90% sure" was a
fair thing to say.

## What is calibration, exactly?

**If Rosa says "90% sure" and it does not rain, was she wrong to say 90%?** No.
One result cannot tell you, because 90% already admits a miss. Only a pile of
"90% sure" calls can.

- **Calibration** means that across many predictions made at the same stated
  confidence, the share that come true matches that number.
- It is a claim about **your confidence**, not your facts. You can know a lot
  and still be badly calibrated.
- **Well-calibrated is not always right.** Rosa at 90% is supposed to miss about
  1 time in 10. Driving the hit rate to 100% is not the goal.

## What are the two ways to miss?

**Ben says 90% and lands 58%. Someone else says 60% and lands 90%. Are they making
the same mistake?** No, they are opposite mistakes, and both are miscalibration.

| Pattern         | Says | Actual hit rate | How it feels to others                            |
| --------------- | ---- | --------------- | ------------------------------------------------- |
| Overconfident   | 90%  | 58% (Ben)       | Sounds certain, and is wrong often enough to hurt |
| Underconfident  | 60%  | 90%             | Hedges everything, so real skill gets discounted  |
| Well-calibrated | 90%  | 89% (Rosa)      | The number can be trusted to mean what it says    |

The same two failures show up in work. An engineer who says "95% sure, ship it"
and is regularly surprised is Ben. One who says "I don't know, maybe 60%" about
things that almost always work out is the opposite, and their team stops
listening to a real signal.

## Where does technical judgment come in?

Estimating how long a task will take, how risky a change is, or how likely a
debugging hypothesis is are all calibration problems wearing work clothes. The
fix is the same small loop Rosa runs each morning: state a number before you
know, find out what happened, compare, adjust. L2 breaks the loop down, and L3
runs it on a real engineering log.
