---
title: "L1 — Why doesn't reading more mean learning more?"
---

- **Exposure** is encountering information — reading it, hearing it, watching it. **Understanding** is being able to use it: apply it to a new problem, explain it without the source in front of you, predict what happens if a condition changes.
- These feel like the same activity from the inside because both produce a subjective sense of familiarity — this is exactly the trap: familiarity ("I recognize this") is not the same signal as competence ("I can produce this from scratch"), but they're easy to confuse in the moment.
- **The fluency illusion**: re-reading a passage makes it feel increasingly clear and obvious, because processing something you've seen before is _easier_, not because you understand it _better_. That ease gets misread as mastery.
- Passive exposure (reading, watching a tutorial, highlighting) is low-effort and produces this illusion reliably. Active recall (closing the material and reproducing it, explaining it out loud, applying it to a new problem) is higher-effort and produces a much more honest signal — it either works or it visibly doesn't.
- The **Feynman technique** operationalizes this: explain a concept in plain language, as if teaching someone with no background, with no peeking at the source. Every point where the explanation breaks down, hand-waves, or reaches for jargon instead of a plain-language mechanism is a precise map of what you don't actually understand yet.
- This isn't an argument against reading or exposure — exposure is a necessary first step, you can't understand what you've never encountered. It's an argument against _stopping_ there and mistaking exposure for the finish line.
- The practical discipline: after any exposure (an article, a course, a code review of unfamiliar code), force a retrieval step before moving on — closing the source and trying to reproduce or apply what was just read, not just nodding along and continuing.
