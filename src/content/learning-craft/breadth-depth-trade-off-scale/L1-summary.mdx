Priya is a staff engineer on the platform team. A VP pings her at 5pm:
a mobile feature team wants to ship a background-sync change tomorrow
morning, and someone senior needs to sign off that it won't tank
battery life for millions of users. Priya has never worked on mobile
power management in her life — she doesn't know what a wakelock is,
let alone how Android schedules background work. **She has until
tomorrow morning. She cannot become a mobile-power expert overnight.
Does that mean she has nothing useful to offer — or is there a
different skill entirely that lets her give a credible answer anyway?**

There genuinely is a third option, and it's not "fake it" or "refuse
the request." It's deciding, fast, whether this specific call actually
needs Priya to personally understand wakelocks — or whether it needs
her to find the one person who already does, ask them a sharp
question, and sanity-check what comes back.

## The shape of the problem

- **Nobody can go deep on everything a staff-level role touches.** The
  role's whole shape — weighing in across databases, mobile, infra,
  security, a dozen services someone else owns — makes personal depth
  everywhere mathematically impossible. Breadth is not a shortcut taken
  under time pressure; it's the only viable default for most decisions,
  and depth is the exception that has to be earned by the stakes of a
  specific call.
- **The two failure modes look opposite but come from the same
  mistake:** treating breadth-vs-depth as an accident of how much time
  happens to be available, instead of a decision made on purpose.
  - Faking depth you don't have (nodding along, guessing at "connection
    pool exhaustion" because it sounds plausible) — this is borrowed
    confidence with no borrowed judgment behind it.
  - Insisting on real depth for a low-stakes, reversible, fast-to-detect
    call that a 20-minute conversation would have settled just as well —
    burning days a decision never needed.
- **The actual skill has two parts, and both are learnable:**
  deciding _where_ breadth is enough versus where real depth is
  required, and running an "ask the right expert" exchange well enough
  that a short conversation replaces days of self-study — without
  swallowing whatever the expert says uncritically.
- **This is the track's synthesis unit.** It leans on judgment skills
  from earlier in this track — weighing conflicting expert opinions,
  staying calibrated about your own confidence — and applies them at
  organizational scale, where the "source" being evaluated is a
  colleague standing in front of you, not a document.

## Key terms

- **Breadth** — working familiarity with a domain: knowing it exists,
  roughly what it does, who owns it, and enough vocabulary to ask a
  sharp question about it — without being able to do the work yourself.
- **Depth** — the ability to personally reason through a domain's
  specifics, verify a claim independently, and make the call without
  relying on anyone else's judgment.
- **Borrowed judgment** — making a decision based on a trusted expert's
  reasoning rather than your own re-derivation of it, while still
  sanity-checking what they told you rather than accepting it blind.
- **Stakes** — how much damage a wrong call in this specific decision
  actually does.
- **Reversibility** — how cheaply a wrong call can be undone once
  discovered.
- **Time-to-signal** — how quickly a wrong call would surface on its
  own, before it causes real damage.
- **Domain expert** — whoever has actually built up real depth in the
  specific area a decision touches, regardless of title.
