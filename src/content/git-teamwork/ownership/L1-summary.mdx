Marcus is on-call. At 2:14 AM, an alert fires: the payments-retry queue
is re-submitting already-successful charges, and customers are being
billed twice. Marcus opens the code and doesn't recognize any of it —
Priya wrote the whole thing alone eight months ago, and no one else
has ever needed to change it. Marcus escalates, pages two more
engineers who also don't recognize the code, and the team spends three
hours guessing at a fix while charges keep duplicating. When Priya
lands and reads one log line, she finds the actual cause and ships a
fix in four minutes. **The fix wasn't hard. What actually cost the
team three hours?**

## The shape of the problem

Marcus didn't lack skill — he lacked **access to what Priya already
knew**. That knowledge lived in exactly one head, and the moment that
head was on a plane, the team's effective ability to fix its own
system dropped to zero. The three-hour outage wasn't a code problem;
it was a knowledge-distribution problem that had been accumulating
for eight months before it ever showed up as an incident.

| What looked like the problem               | What was actually the problem                          |
| ------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| Marcus was slow to find the bug            | Marcus had never seen this code before tonight         |
| The team escalated too many times          | Nobody else had context to escalate _to_               |
| The fix took 3 hours                       | The fix took 4 minutes; discovery took the other 2h56m |
| Priya is "just really good" at this system | Priya is the _only_ input to this system               |

## Key terms

- **Bus factor** — the number of people who could disappear (quit,
  get sick, go on vacation) before a system becomes un-maintainable.
  A bus factor of 1 means exactly one person's absence stops the
  team cold — which is exactly what happened at 2:14 AM.
- **Single-owner model** — one person is the de facto (or explicit)
  owner of a piece of the codebase; changes and incident response
  route through them by default.
- **Collective ownership** — anyone on the team can and does modify
  any part of the codebase; no single person is a bottleneck, but
  consistency and depth of expertise take more deliberate effort to
  maintain.
- **On-call rotation** — a scheduled, shared responsibility for
  responding to production incidents, so no single person is always
  the one paged (and, more importantly here, so incident response
  doesn't depend on one specific person's memory).
- **Runbook** — a written, checked-in procedure for diagnosing and
  responding to a specific class of incident, meant to let _whoever
  is on-call_, not just the original author, act with real context.
- **Knowledge sharing** — the deliberate practices (pairing, rotating
  reviewers, documentation, walkthroughs) that spread understanding
  of a system across more than one person before an incident forces
  the issue.

## What this unit covers

L2 works through the actual trade-off between ownership models —
single-owner isn't simply "wrong" and collective isn't simply
"right," each has a real cost — and what a working on-call rotation
needs beyond just a pager schedule. L3 walks the payments-retry
incident in full, including the runbook, escalation path, and
ownership-model change the team actually made afterward, and compares
that against a second, harder case where collective ownership was the
wrong call.
