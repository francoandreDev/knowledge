## The deal: Dana and Marcus

Dana sells a dispatch-scheduling tool. Marcus runs operations at
Fairmont Logistics, where trucks and drivers are currently assigned
using a shared spreadsheet three dispatchers edit by hand. Dana opens
with a short discovery question, and Marcus answers with the objection
this unit's title names first.

**Marcus:** Look, I'll be upfront — we already have something that
works. The spreadsheet's not pretty, but it does the job.

**Dana:** That's fair, and honestly a lot of teams run on exactly that
kind of system for years. Is there anything about it that still costs
you time or money, even though it's basically working?

_Acknowledge, then isolate — Dana doesn't defend the product or attack
the spreadsheet. The question is genuinely open: it's asking Marcus to
supply the answer, not confirming one Dana already assumes._

**Marcus:** ...Actually, yeah. Two dispatchers editing it at once is a
problem. Twice last month we double-booked the same truck for two
routes, and someone had to scramble a replacement or eat the overtime.

**Dana:** How much does that actually cost when it happens?

**Marcus:** Rough guess — maybe $2,100 in overtime and rush coverage,
each time it happens. It's happened four times this quarter.

_That's roughly $8,400 this quarter, on Marcus's own number, not
Dana's. Dana didn't have to argue the spreadsheet was bad — Marcus
just described a real, costly gap in it, unprompted, once asked
directly instead of argued with._

**Dana:** That's the exact failure mode our scheduling tool is built
to prevent — it locks a truck the moment it's assigned, so two people
can't double-book it by accident. Want me to walk through how that
would have caught last month's two incidents specifically?

**Marcus:** Sure, show me.

<Checkpoint question="Dana's reframe at the end is one short sentence pointing at a specific capability, not a full feature tour. Why does that work here, when a longer, more thorough pitch might seem safer?">

It works because the reframe only has one job left to do at this
point: connect a capability to a cost Marcus already named himself.
Dana doesn't need to convince Marcus a problem exists or that it's
worth solving — Marcus just supplied both of those, in his own words,
by answering the isolate question honestly. A longer pitch here would
actually work against Dana: it would re-open territory ("here's
everything we do") that Marcus doesn't need convincing on, and risk
burying the one capability that matters under nine others that don't.
The short reframe works precisely because the acknowledge and isolate
steps already did the harder work of finding out what to point at.

</Checkpoint>

## Later in the same call: the price objection

The demo lands well. Dana quotes list price: $24,000/year. Marcus goes
quiet, then says the second objection this unit's title names.

**Marcus:** Honestly, that's too expensive for us right now.

**Dana:** Totally fair to raise — can I ask, is $24,000 outside what's
actually budgeted, or is it more that you're not sure yet it's worth
that much given what you've seen?

_Same acknowledge → isolate move, aimed at the actual fork in a price
objection: a real budget ceiling needs a different response than an
unconvinced value case does, and guessing wrong wastes the rest of the
conversation._

**Marcus:** No, it's real — we've got about $18,000 approved for
something in this category this year. Above that, I'd need to go back
to finance, and that's a conversation I'd rather not have unless I'm
confident it's worth it.

**Dana:** Understood — and just so I'm reasoning about the right
number: the double-booking problem alone is running you around $8,400
a quarter, so roughly $33,600 a year if nothing changes. Even at list
price, this pays for itself close to three times over just on that one
failure mode, before anything else it catches. Given that, could we
make $18,000 work for a shorter first term, or is there a different
way to close that six-thousand-dollar gap?

_This is the counter-anchor: Dana doesn't just repeat "$24,000" or cave
straight to Marcus's number. Restating the cost Marcus already
confirmed resets the conversation around value instead of around who
blinks first on the list price._

**Marcus:** If you could get closer to $18,000, I could probably get
this approved without a finance meeting. What would you need from us
to make that work?

**Dana:** Here's what I can do — $19,800 a year, which is a little
over your budget, but locked in for two years instead of one, so the
per-year number holds even if list price goes up next year. In
exchange, I'd want two things: the two-year term itself, and if the
tool performs the way we both expect after the first quarter, would
you be open to a short reference call with a prospect in a similar
spot to where you are now?

**Marcus:** Two years is fine if the price is locked, and yeah, I can
do a reference call if it's actually working for us by then. Let's do
it.

_This is concession trading, not a discount. Dana gave up $4,200/year
in list price. In exchange, Dana got a two-year commitment (reducing
the risk of losing the account after one year) and a future reference
(a real sales asset for other deals) — not a one-way giveaway that
would have trained Marcus to expect a lower number again at renewal
for nothing back._

<Checkpoint question="Dana's final number, $19,800, is still above Marcus's stated $18,000 budget — and Marcus said yes anyway. What made that possible, given that isolate had already confirmed $18,000 was a real ceiling?">

The $18,000 figure was real as a number Marcus could approve without
an extra finance conversation, not as an absolute ceiling on what the
deal could ever cost. Two things changed what Marcus was willing to
accept: the reasoning Dana attached to $19,800 (tying it to a
value Marcus had already agreed was real, and to protection against a
future price increase, not just "please pay more"), and the fact that
the number came bundled with terms — a locked two-year rate — that
made $19,800 arguably a better deal over time than $18,000 for one
year at a price that could rise later. A number restated with reasoning
and packaged with a real trade can move a genuine budget line in a way
that a bare request to pay more never would.

</Checkpoint>

## The same two moments, run by an untrained rep

Here's what this exact call looked like when Theo, a rep who skips
acknowledge and isolate under pressure, got the identical lead.

**Marcus:** Look, I'll be upfront — we already have something that
works. The spreadsheet's not pretty, but it does the job.

**Theo:** Sure, but a spreadsheet can't stop double-entry the way our
tool can, and you don't get real-time visibility, and there's no audit
trail if something goes wrong, and —

**Marcus:** We've been fine without those. I should probably let you
go.

Theo never got to hear about the $8,400-a-quarter overtime problem,
because arguing the spreadsheet's flaws put Marcus in a position of
defending a decision he'd already made, rather than describing what
still bothered him about it. Suppose, generously, the call had somehow
continued anyway to a price conversation:

**Theo:** I hear you on the budget — I can do $18,000 flat, right now,
no strings attached.

This looks like the same number Dana eventually landed near. It isn't
the same outcome. Theo never confirmed whether $18,000 was a genuine
ceiling or an opening move, gave up the full $6,000 with nothing asked
in return, and signaled — by discounting instantly, unprompted — that
the $24,000 number was never real to begin with. Even if this
particular deal closed, Theo has now trained Marcus (and, by
reputation, anyone Marcus talks to) to always ask for a discount first,
and has no two-year lock-in or reference call to show for the $6,000
given away. The two calls used almost the same words at the end and
produced very different deals.

## What changes if the objection is a smokescreen?

Not every "we already have something that works" is really about the
spreadsheet. Suppose Marcus's isolate answer had instead been: "Not
really — it's fine. I just need to check with my director before
moving on anything new." That's not a rejection of the product; it's a
different real blocker (approval authority) wearing the same opening
line. The reframe that follows has to change accordingly — pitching
harder at a spreadsheet-versus-tool comparison would waste the rest of
the call on a concern Marcus never actually had. The right move is to
isolate again, one level deeper: "Understood — what would your
director want to know to feel good about it?" This turns a vague
stall into a concrete, addressable step (a one-page cost summary for
the director, say) instead of a rebuttal aimed at a problem that was
never real. The acknowledge → isolate → reframe structure doesn't
change; what changes is recognizing, from the isolate answer itself,
that the real blocker isn't the one named first.

## What changes if the budget constraint turns out to be genuinely unmovable?

Suppose isolate on the price objection had gone differently: "It's not
just this year's line item — the whole category got frozen after a
bad quarter, and nothing above $10,000 is getting approved by anyone,
full stop, for the rest of the year." That's a different kind of
"real" than Marcus's actual $18,000 ceiling — not a number that can
move with the right reasoning or trade, but a hard constraint set
somewhere Dana has no leverage over. Reframing harder or trading more
generously doesn't fix a frozen budget; it just spends more of Dana's
margin chasing a deal that legally cannot close this cycle no matter
what's said. The correct move at that point is the negotiation basic
L2 named last: walking away from this deal, this quarter, is the
right outcome — not a failure of the acknowledge → isolate → reframe
structure, but the structure doing its job by surfacing, honestly,
that there is nothing left to negotiate here right now. The better
close in that situation is often not a smaller number but a real next
step that isn't a sale: "Let's put a placeholder in for when the
freeze lifts, and I'll check back in with you then" — leaving the
relationship open instead of pressuring a prospect toward a yes that
finance would reject anyway, which would cost more in trust than the
deal is worth.

**One more variation worth sitting with:** what would change in this
same call if Marcus weren't the actual budget owner at all, and the
$18,000 he quoted turned out to be his own guess rather than a
confirmed number? The isolate question that surfaces that — "is that
number something you've confirmed, or your best estimate of what's
approved?" — matters just as much as the ones asked above, because a
concession trade built on a guessed number can fall apart entirely
once it reaches whoever actually controls the budget. The worked call
above is one path through a real deal, not the only shape an
objection-handling conversation can take — the habit worth keeping is
asking what's still unconfirmed, not assuming the first number given
is the final one.
