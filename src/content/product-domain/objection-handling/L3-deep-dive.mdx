Dana sells a dispatch-scheduling tool. Marcus runs operations at Fairmont
Logistics, where trucks and drivers are assigned through a spreadsheet that
three dispatchers edit by hand. List price is $24,000 a year, and Marcus has
about $18,000 approved for this category. Same facts as L1 and L2; L3 walks the
actual call, the contrast with an untrained rep, and two variations.

**Before reading on: Marcus will raise two objections. Which one do you
expect to be the harder to handle well, and why?**

## First objection: "we already have something that works"

**Marcus:** I'll be upfront. We already have something that works. The
spreadsheet's not pretty, but it does the job.

**Dana:** That's fair, and plenty of teams run on exactly that for years. Is
there anything about it that still costs you time or money, even though it
basically works?

_Acknowledge, then isolate. Dana neither defends the product nor attacks the
spreadsheet. The question is open: it asks Marcus to supply the answer._

**Marcus:** Actually, yes. Two dispatchers editing at once is a problem. We
double-booked the same truck four times this quarter, and each time someone
scrambled a replacement or ate the overtime.

**Dana:** Roughly what does it cost when it happens?

**Marcus:** Maybe $2,100 each time, in overtime and rush coverage.

That is 4 × $2,100 = $8,400 this quarter, on Marcus's own number. Dana did not
have to argue that the spreadsheet was bad.

**Dana:** That's the failure our tool is built to prevent. It locks a truck the
moment it is assigned, so two people cannot book it by accident. Want me to walk
through how it would have caught last month's two incidents?

**Marcus:** Sure, show me.

<Checkpoint question="Dana's reframe is one sentence pointing at one capability, not a feature tour. Why is that enough here?">

By answering the isolate question, Marcus already supplied both things a pitch
usually has to establish: that a problem exists and that it costs real money.
The reframe only has to connect one capability to a cost he named himself. A
longer tour would reopen ground Marcus does not need convincing on and bury the
one thing that matters under nine that do not.

</Checkpoint>

## Second objection: "that's too expensive"

The demo lands. Dana quotes $24,000 a year, and Marcus goes quiet.

**Marcus:** Honestly, that's too expensive for us right now.

**Dana:** Fair to raise. Is $24,000 outside what is budgeted, or are you not yet
sure it is worth that much given what you have seen?

_Same acknowledge-and-isolate move, aimed at the fork inside a price objection.
A real ceiling needs a different reply than an unconvinced value case does._

**Marcus:** It's real. About $18,000 is approved for this category this year.
Above that I would need to go back to finance, and I'd rather not unless I am
confident.

**Dana:** Understood. To reason about the right number: the double-booking
problem alone is running about $8,400 a quarter, so roughly $33,600 a year if
nothing changes. Even at list price the tool would cost less than the problem.
Could we make $18,000 work for a shorter first term, or find another way to
close the $6,000 gap?

This is a counter-anchor with reasoning. Dana neither repeats "$24,000" nor
jumps to Marcus's number; restating the cost Marcus confirmed puts the value
back at the centre. (The annual figure assumes the current rate of incidents
continues, which is an assumption, not a promise.)

**Marcus:** If you got closer to $18,000, I could probably get this approved
without a finance meeting. What would you need from us?

**Dana:** $19,800 a year, locked for two years so the rate holds if list price
goes up. In exchange I would want the two-year term itself, and, if it performs
as we both expect after the first quarter, a short reference call with a
similar prospect.

**Marcus:** Two years is fine if the price is locked, and I can do a reference
call if it is actually working. Let's do it.

|                    | List    | Dana's deal                            | Theo's version (below) |
| ------------------ | ------- | -------------------------------------- | ---------------------- |
| Price per year     | $24,000 | $19,800                                | $18,000                |
| Discount off list  | none    | $4,200 (17.5%)                         | $6,000 (25%)           |
| Term               | 1 year  | 2 years, rate locked                   | 1 year                 |
| Received in return | none    | Two-year commitment + future reference | Nothing                |

Dana's discount is smaller and comes with two things back. The interactive demo
on this unit models the lock-in: with an assumed 6% yearly list increase, a
17.5% discount over a two-year term has a modeled effective annual value of
about $20,520, higher than the $19,800 headline, because the lock protects
against an increase that would otherwise apply in year two. The 6% is an
assumption of the model, not a measured figure.

<Checkpoint question="$19,800 is still above Marcus's stated $18,000, and he said yes. What made that possible after isolate confirmed $18,000 was real?">

The $18,000 was real as the amount Marcus could approve without a finance
conversation, not an absolute ceiling on what the deal could cost. Two things
moved him: reasoning tied to a cost he had already agreed was real, and terms,
a locked two-year rate, that made $19,800 arguably a better deal over time than
$18,000 for one year with no protection. A number restated with reasons and
packaged as a trade can move a genuine budget line that a bare request to pay
more could not.

</Checkpoint>

## The same two moments, with an untrained rep

Theo gets the identical lead and skips acknowledge and isolate under pressure.

**Marcus:** We already have something that works. The spreadsheet's not pretty,
but it does the job.

**Theo:** Sure, but a spreadsheet can't stop double entry, and you don't get
real-time visibility, and there's no audit trail, and...

**Marcus:** We've been fine without those. I should probably let you go.

Theo never hears about the $8,400-a-quarter problem, because arguing put Marcus
in the position of defending his setup. If the call somehow reached price:

**Theo:** I hear you on budget. I can do $18,000 flat, right now, no strings.

The number is even lower than Dana's $19,800, and the deal is worse. Theo never
checked whether $18,000 was a real ceiling or an opening move, gave up $6,000 a
year with nothing asked back, and signalled that $24,000 was never the real
price. Even if this deal closes, the next negotiation with Marcus starts from
"always ask for a discount first."

## What changes if the objection is a smokescreen?

**What if Marcus's answer to "does anything still cost you time?" had been
"not really, I just need to check with my director before anything new"?** Then
the stated objection was not about the spreadsheet at all. Approval authority
was the real blocker wearing the same opening line, and pitching harder at the
spreadsheet would spend the call on a concern Marcus never had. The model does
not change; the answer to isolate changes the next question: "What would your
director want to know to feel good about it?" That turns a vague stall into a
concrete step, such as a one-page cost summary.

## What changes if the budget genuinely cannot move?

**What if the answer had been "the whole category was frozen after a bad
quarter, and nothing above $10,000 gets approved by anyone this year"?** That
is a different kind of real. $18,000 could move with the right reasoning and
terms; a freeze is a constraint set somewhere Dana has no leverage over, and
trading harder only spends margin on a deal that cannot close this cycle.
Walking away from this deal, this quarter, is the model doing its job by
surfacing that there is nothing left to negotiate. The better close is a
next step that is not a sale: a placeholder for when the freeze lifts, leaving
the relationship open.

| Stated objection                                 | What isolate found                         | Reply that fit                                                 |
| ------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| "We already have something that works"           | $8,400 a quarter in double-booking costs   | Point at the one capability that prevents it                   |
| "It's too expensive"                             | A real $18,000 approval line               | Counter-anchor on value, then trade                            |
| "We already have something that works" (variant) | Needs director approval, not product doubt | Isolate one level deeper; give the director a concrete summary |
| "It's too expensive" (variant)                   | A frozen category                          | Walk away this cycle; keep the relationship open               |

Two rows share an opening line and have different real blockers, which is why
isolate has to run every time and not only when an objection sounds unusual.

<Checkpoint question="In the frozen-budget variant, why is walking away a success of the method rather than a failure of it?">

Acknowledge and isolate exist to find out what is actually true. If what is true
is that no amount of reasoning or trading can produce a yes this cycle, the
method has delivered its most valuable result: it stopped Dana from spending
margin and trust on an impossible close. Pressing on would likely cost more in
the relationship than the deal is worth, and a placeholder for when the freeze
lifts preserves the chance of a real deal later.

</Checkpoint>

## Extend the case

**This is one path through one deal, not the whole territory.** Try changing it:
what would change if Marcus were not the budget owner at all, and the $18,000
turned out to be his own guess rather than a confirmed number? What if the
buyer's incident costs were much smaller than $2,100? What if the buyer asked
for a discount, and the only thing you could take back was something they did
not value? A good isolate question here is "is that number confirmed, or your
best estimate of what is approved?", because a trade built on a guessed number
can collapse when it reaches whoever really controls the budget. The habit
worth keeping is asking what is still unconfirmed, not assuming the first
number given is the final one.
