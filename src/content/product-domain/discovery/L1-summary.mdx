A patient walks in and says: "I've had this cough for weeks. I need
antibiotics." Doctor A takes that sentence as the whole diagnosis and
writes the prescription. The cough does not improve.

Doctor B says: "Before I prescribe anything, tell me about the cough.
When does it get worse?" The patient mentions it is mostly at night and
after big meals, with no fever at all. That points somewhere an
antibiotic cannot reach, and B treats that instead.

The patient never lied to either doctor. "I need antibiotics" was
simply the patient's best guess at the cause, said out loud.

**Both doctors heard the same sentence. What did Doctor B do that A did
not, and why did it change the treatment?**

</Scenario>

Doctor B treated the request as a first guess and spent a minute
finding out what was true. Selling to a business works the same way:
a prospect's stated ask ("pricing for a tool for 8 people") is the
patient's "I need antibiotics". It is how they currently see the
problem, not always the problem itself. This unit is about running
that fact-finding conversation on purpose, in a sales or client
setting, instead of getting lucky at it.

## What is the difference between pitching and discovery?

**Two reps get the same inbound message: "We are an 8-person team and
want pricing for a project tool." One answers it directly, the other
asks first. Why do the deals come out so differently?** Here are both
calls side by side. (Names and figures are made up for teaching.)

|                        | Adri (pitches first)               | Bex (asks first)                                     |
| ---------------------- | ---------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| First words            | "Let me show you what we do"       | "What is driving the search right now?"              |
| Who talks most         | The rep                            | The prospect                                         |
| What the call finds    | Nothing new                        | Three teams hand work off, and handoffs keep failing |
| What the prospect says | "Looks nice, we will look it over" | "Avoiding another missed launch is worth far more"   |
| Deal                   | Nothing closes                     | 24-seat, three-team plan at $28,800 a year           |

The prospect, Priya, originally asked about an 8-seat plan at $4,800 a
year. Bex closed six times that, because the deal size follows the
size of the real problem, not the size of the first request.

- A **pitch** is the seller talking: here is what we do and what it
  costs. **Discovery** reverses it: the seller asks, the prospect talks,
  and the seller listens closely enough to understand a situation they
  did not live through.
- **Pitching too early** is a common way for a deal to end up smaller
  or lost. The pitch can be accurate and still answer a question nobody
  has checked is the real one.

## Which kinds of questions open a conversation?

**"Do you need reporting?" and "What happens today when a handoff gets
missed?" are both questions. Which one is likelier to teach the seller
something new?** The second, because the first has the seller's guess
built into it.

|                       | Closed question                      | Open question                                           |
| --------------------- | ------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| Example               | "Is 8 seats the right number?"       | "Walk me through who touches the work after your team." |
| Produces              | Yes, no or a number                  | A story with details the seller did not know to ask for |
| Where the guess lives | In the wording of the question       | Nowhere: the prospect supplies it                       |
| Best used             | To confirm a detail late in the call | To surface the real problem early                       |

Closed questions are not wrong. They are the right tool once the real
problem is understood and a detail needs confirming. Used first, they
only get the prospect nodding along to the seller's first guess.

## How do you keep it from becoming an interrogation?

A light four-step order gives the questions a shape: **situation**
(what is the setup today), **problem** (what is the friction in it),
**implication** (what does that friction cost) and **need-payoff**
(what would solving it be worth, said by the prospect). L2 takes each
step apart, and L3 runs a whole call.

```mermaid
flowchart LR
    A["Situation"] --> B["Problem"] --> C["Implication"] --> D["Need-payoff"]
```

The aim is not more questions. It is a handful of genuinely open ones,
and listening for the gap between the stated problem and its real cost.
