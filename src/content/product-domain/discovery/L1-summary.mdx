Two reps get the same inbound lead within twenty minutes of each other: a
message from Priya, an ops manager at a small studio, saying "Hi, we're
an 8-person team and we're looking for pricing on a project management
tool." Adri takes the call, and since the ask is already stated, walks
straight into the standard pitch — fifteen minutes of screen share,
feature by feature. Priya says "Thanks, this looks nice, we'll look it
over," and that's the last anyone hears from her.

Bex takes the identical lead and, before showing a single screen, asks:
"Before I show you anything — what's actually driving the search for a
tool right now?" Three more questions later, it turns out the real
problem isn't "we need a tool for 8 people" at all — it's that Priya's
team and two other teams they hand work off to keep missing client
deadlines because handoffs fall through the cracks, and it's already
costing them in rush fees. Bex doesn't sell the small 8-seat starter
plan Priya originally asked about. Bex sells a 24-seat, multi-team
coordination plan — six times the size of the deal Priya thought she
was shopping for.

**Both reps had the same lead, the same product, and roughly the same
amount of talk time. What did the two calls actually differ in, and why
did it change the deal size six-fold?**

</Scenario>

The difference wasn't product knowledge, energy, or charisma — both
reps knew the product equally well. The difference was that Adri
treated the stated ask as the whole problem and answered it, while Bex
treated the stated ask as a first guess and spent the call finding out
what was actually true. Priya never lied — she genuinely believed "a
project tool for 8 people" was what she needed, because that was the
shape of the problem as she experienced it day to day. She just hadn't
been asked the questions that would have surfaced the bigger, real
one. This unit is about running that conversation on purpose, instead
of getting lucky at it.

## The shape of the problem

- A **pitch** is a conversation where the seller talks and the buyer
  listens: here's what we do, here's what it costs. **Discovery** is
  the opposite structure — the seller asks, the buyer talks, and the
  seller's job is to listen closely enough to understand a situation
  they didn't live through.
- **Pitching too early is the single most common way a deal gets
  smaller than it could have been, or lost outright.** It's not that
  the pitch was bad — it's that it answered a question the prospect
  asked in their own limited words, before anyone found out whether
  that was the real question. A confident answer to the wrong problem
  still loses.
- **Closed questions** ("Do you need reporting?", "Is 8 seats
  right?") produce a yes/no and confirm what the seller already
  assumes. **Open questions** ("What's driving this search right
  now?", "What happens today when a handoff gets missed?") produce a
  story, and stories are where the real, often-bigger problem lives —
  a prospect rarely volunteers it unprompted, because they usually
  haven't framed it that way themselves yet.
- A lightweight, four-step question progression — **situation,
  problem, implication, need-payoff** — gives structure to a
  discovery conversation without turning it into an interrogation:
  first understand the current setup, then the specific friction in
  it, then what that friction actually costs if left alone, then let
  the prospect say out loud what solving it would be worth. L2 works
  through each step; L3 runs the full conversation end to end.
- The point isn't to ask more questions for their own sake — a
  ten-minute interrogation is its own failure mode. It's to ask a
  handful of genuinely open ones, listen for the gap between the
  stated problem and its real cost, and let that gap shape the pitch
  that comes after — not the other way around.
