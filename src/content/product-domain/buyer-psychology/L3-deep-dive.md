title: "L3 — The follow-up call that finds the real objection"
---

## The follow-up, three weeks later

**Before anything else — what does the vendor actually ask, and why
that specific question?** Not "did you make a decision," which
invites a yes/no stall, and not "is something else going on," which
sounds like an accusation:

> Vendor: "Totally understand needing time. Can I ask — what would
> need to be true for this to be an easy yes on your end?"
>
> Client: _(pause)_ "...Honestly? The tool itself isn't the issue.
> My concern is that I'm the one who'd be recommending we switch
> monitoring vendors, and if the migration causes any disruption in
> the first month, that's on me — even though the current setup is
> genuinely worse."

"Migration" here means moving from one tool to another. "Rollout"
means the period when the new tool is introduced into real work. The
client is worried about the messy middle: the old system is imperfect,
but everyone knows how to survive it; the new system might be better,
but the switch itself could create blame.

**What did this question actually surface that the original pitch
never touched?** The real objection was never about the product —
it was about the client's personal exposure if a switch they
personally championed went badly during rollout. The stated
objection ("we'll think about it") gave no hint of this; it took a
forward-looking, non-accusatory question to surface it.

## What changes once the real objection is known

**Given this new information, does the vendor go back to pitching
product features?** No — that would ignore exactly what was just
uncovered. The real objection is about _rollout risk to the client
personally_, not about the tool's capability, so the response has
to address that specifically:

> Vendor: "That makes sense — and it's a completely reasonable thing
> to be cautious about. What if we ran this as a parallel pilot
> first? Keep your current monitoring running exactly as-is, stand
> up ours alongside it for three weeks with zero changes to your
> existing alerts, and only cut over once you've personally seen it
> catch everything the old one does. That way there's no month where
> you're exposed if something's missed."

A parallel pilot means trying the new thing beside the old thing,
without turning the old thing off. "Cut over" means making the new
tool the real one people depend on. In this offer, the client gets to
compare both systems before any risky switch happens.

**Why does this specific offer directly answer the real objection,
where a discount or a features list wouldn't have?** The client's
concern was never price or capability — it was "if I recommend this
and it fails during the transition, that's my exposure." A parallel
pilot with zero changes to the existing setup removes exactly that
risk: nothing about the current monitoring changes until the client
has personally verified the new tool works, which means there's no
window where switching could cause the disruption they were afraid
of.

## Why the original pitch couldn't have found this on its own

**Could a better first pitch have surfaced this concern without a
follow-up call?** Not reliably — the client likely didn't consciously
frame their own hesitation as "reputational risk from rollout
timing" until asked a question that required them to articulate what
was actually missing. Buyer psychology isn't always a hidden truth
the buyer is deliberately withholding; sometimes the buyer themselves
hasn't put it into words yet, and the right question is what
surfaces it for both people at once.

## What generalizes and what doesn't

The pattern — a stated objection about the product usually sits on
top of a real concern about the buyer's own risk or authority, and a
forward-looking question ("what would need to be true") surfaces it
without accusation — generalizes to most B2B sales conversations and
plenty of internal ones (getting a manager to approve a project,
convincing a teammate to adopt a new tool). What's specific to this
worked example: the exact real objection (personal rollout risk) and
the exact fix (a zero-risk parallel pilot) are this client's
situation — a different stalled deal might reveal a budget-authority
problem, or an internal-politics problem, needing a completely
different response. **Try extending it yourself:** if the "what
would need to be true" question had instead revealed "I actually
don't have the authority to approve this — my boss does, and she
hasn't seen this yet," would the parallel-pilot offer from this
scenario still be the right response, or does a different kind of
real objection need a structurally different kind of fix?

A better response in that authority case would sound different:
"Would it help if we prepared a short internal summary for your boss,
joined a decision call, or mapped the approval steps together?" The
fix is no longer mainly reducing rollout risk; it is helping the
buyer involve the person who can actually approve the purchase.

## Failure modes

| Failure mode                                                                                       | What it gets wrong                                                                                                                                                                                                     |
| -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Responding to "we'll think about it" with more product features or a bigger discount               | If the real objection is personal risk or lack of authority, neither features nor price addresses it — the response has to match the actual concern, not the stated one                                                |
| Asking "is that the real reason?" instead of a forward-looking question                            | Puts the buyer on the defensive and assumes dishonesty, making them less likely to actually share the real concern                                                                                                     |
| Assuming the real objection once found is fixed and doesn't need a tailored response               | Surfacing the real concern is only useful if the response actually addresses it — the parallel pilot worked because it specifically removed the rollout-risk exposure, not because the concern was merely acknowledged |
| Treating this one scenario's answer (a parallel pilot) as the universal fix for every stalled deal | The right response depends entirely on what the real objection turns out to be — a pilot solves rollout risk, but does nothing for a budget-authority problem or an internal-politics problem                          |
