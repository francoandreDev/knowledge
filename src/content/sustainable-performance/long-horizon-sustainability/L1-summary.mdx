An engineer has just made staff after eight years, the last three of
them spent at close to 55 hours a week, absorbing whichever launch was
labeled "critical" that quarter. Every individual quarter looked like a
win on paper — shipped on time, well reviewed, promotion-track feedback.
And yet the week the promotion is announced, there's no relief, just a
flat, tired "now what," and a new and unfamiliar reluctance to open the
laptop on Monday morning. **Each quarter, taken alone, was a genuinely
good quarter. So why does three years of good quarters add up to
something that feels like the opposite of sustainable?**

## The shape of the problem

- Optimizing every single quarter for maximum output is not the same
  thing as optimizing a **decade** for maximum output — a sequence of
  locally-maximal quarters can still add up to a career trajectory that
  breaks down, because the thing that makes any one quarter look good
  (running hot, skipping recovery, treating everything as urgent) is
  exactly the thing that makes a decade of them unsustainable.
- **Long-horizon sustainability** is the practice of pacing effort
  across years the way a marathon runner paces a race, not the way a
  sprinter paces a 100m dash — deliberately varying intensity over
  time, with real recovery built in on purpose, rather than running
  every stretch at the same "give it everything" pace and hoping the
  body (or the career) holds out.
- Staff-level output isn't produced by raw hours sustained
  indefinitely — it depends on judgment, mentoring, and cross-team
  trust that only accumulate when there's enough slack to reflect,
  teach, and notice what's about to go wrong before it does. A
  permanently redlined engineer has less of that capacity available,
  not more, even while logging more hours.

## Key terms

- **Sustainable pace** — a level of intensity a person can hold
  indefinitely without accumulating a recovery debt that eventually has
  to be paid, usually at a worse rate than it was borrowed.
- **Seasons model** — treating a career as alternating deliberate
  seasons of higher-intensity push and genuine lower-intensity recovery,
  each planned in advance, rather than one undifferentiated stretch of
  "as much as I can, for as long as I can."
- **Recovery debt** — the gap between the recovery a stretch of
  high-intensity work actually required and the recovery it got; unpaid
  debt doesn't vanish, it compounds and eventually forces itself due,
  usually as a crash rather than a scheduled rest.
- **Decade-horizon thinking** — evaluating a decision ("take this
  project," "skip this vacation," "always be the one who stays late")
  by its effect on ten years of output and reputation, not just the
  current quarter's review cycle.

## What this unit covers

L2 works out why a string of good quarters can still fail at the decade
scale, and what the seasons model actually requires to work — deliberate
timing, not just occasional rest. L3 walks one engineer's full decade,
including the near-burnout inflection point this Scenario opens on, and
what changed afterward: what they adjusted, what it cost, and what it
would have looked like to handle it worse.
