A runner decides to train for ten years, and every single day she runs as hard
as she possibly can. Look at any one day and it is a great run: fast, focused,
a clear step beyond an easier day. Look at a whole season and her times get
slower, her legs ache before she starts, and the thing that was fun has become
something she dreads. **Each day was the best choice on its own. So why does
ten years of best choices add up to the opposite of a strong decade?**

## The shape of the problem

- A run of individually good choices can still add up to a bad long-term
  result, because the cost of "running hot" is paid later, not in the quarter
  that caused it.
- **Long-horizon sustainability** means pacing effort over years the way a
  marathon runner paces a race, not the way a sprinter paces a dash: effort
  varies on purpose, and rest is part of the plan.
- A hard push is not the mistake. Treating _every_ stretch as a push, with no
  planned stretch to pay it back, is.

## Key terms

| Term                    | Meaning                                                                       | Everyday version                                                    |
| ----------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Sustainable pace        | An intensity you can hold for years without a hidden bill                     | The easy-run speed you could keep up all week                       |
| Seasons model           | Alternating planned push stretches and planned recovery stretches             | Training block, then a lighter week                                 |
| Recovery debt           | The gap between recovery a push needed and recovery it got; it compounds      | Skipping sleep: the tiredness does not vanish, it comes back larger |
| Decade-horizon thinking | Judging a choice by what it does to ten years of output, not one review cycle | Asking "can I do this every week?" instead of "can I do it today?"  |

## What this unit covers

L2 works out why strong quarters can still fail as a decade, and what the
seasons model needs to actually work (rest planned before the push, not
"someday"). L3 follows one engineer's full decade, including the point where
it nearly broke and what changed afterward.
