---
title: "L1 — Why does working more hours eventually produce less, not more?"
---

- The intuitive model is **linear**: output = hours × some constant rate, so working 20% more hours should produce roughly 20% more output. Real output curves are not linear — they show **diminishing returns**, and past a certain point, **negative returns**, where more hours produce _less_ total output than fewer hours would have.
- The mechanism isn't mysterious: focus, judgment, and error rate all degrade with fatigue and time-on-task — a tired hour doesn't just produce less than a fresh hour, it can actively produce _negative_ value (a bug introduced that costs more hours to fix than the extra hour saved).
- **Diminishing returns** means each additional hour produces less marginal output than the one before it — output is still increasing, just slower. **Negative returns** is the point past that where an additional hour actually _reduces_ total output, because the mistakes and rework it causes outweigh what it directly produces.
- This isn't primarily a wellness argument — the case here is one of throughput, made in the same terms as any other engineering trade-off: if the goal is maximum sustained output over a real time horizon (a quarter, a year), pushing past the diminishing-returns point is a measurably worse strategy than the sustainable pace, not a nicer-but-slower one.
- The curve's exact shape differs by task type (deep, error-prone technical work degrades faster than routine, low-stakes work) and by time horizon (a single all-nighter before a hard deadline is a different trade than a chronic 60-hour week sustained for months) — this unit is about the shape of the relationship, not a universal "N hours is the correct number."
- The practical implication: "we need this done faster" and "add more hours" are not the same lever past a certain point — sustained overtime is a real cost with a real, measurable ceiling on what it can buy, and treating it as free capacity is a modeling error, not a matter of discipline or effort.
