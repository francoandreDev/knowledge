---
title: 'L1 — Why does "if it''s not A, it must be B" often fail?'
---

- A **fallacy** is a specific, nameable pattern of flawed reasoning — naming one isn't pedantry, it's precision: "that's a false dichotomy" identifies exactly what's wrong, where "that doesn't sound right" doesn't.
- **False dichotomy** (the unit's title example): presenting two options as the only possibilities when a third (or more) genuinely exists. "If it's not the database, it must be the network" quietly assumes those are the only two possible causes, ruling out application-code bugs, caching, client behavior, and more, without ever actually ruling them out.
- **Straw man**: arguing against a weaker, distorted version of someone's actual position, then treating that as if it refuted the real one — feels like winning the argument, but the real position was never addressed.
- **Ad hominem**: attacking the person making a claim instead of the claim itself. "You would say that, you built that system" might be true and might even be _relevant context_ — but it doesn't address whether the claim is correct, which is the part that actually matters.
- **Appeal to authority (misused)**: citing someone's authority on a completely different domain as if it settles a claim in this one — a brilliant systems architect's opinion on a UX question isn't automatically more correct than anyone else's, because expertise doesn't transfer across domains by default.
- **Slippery slope**: claiming a small first step inevitably leads to an extreme outcome, without establishing the actual mechanism connecting each step — "if we allow one exception, we'll have to allow every exception" is only valid if there's a real reason the first exception can't be bounded, not just an assertion that it can't.
- These patterns recur specifically because they're _persuasive_ independent of being _valid_ — this unit's practical value is being able to name the exact move happening in a real technical or organizational argument, which is what lets you respond to the actual structural problem instead of just feeling like something's off.
