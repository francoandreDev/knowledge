A platform team has been arguing for three weeks about whether to split
their monolith into services now, or keep it one deployable unit with
strong internal module boundaries. Every meeting ends the same way:
Priya pushes for splitting now ("we can't ship independently otherwise"),
Marcus pushes back ("that's three new failure domains we don't have
on-call maturity for yet"), Dana raises a third objection ("new hires
already take six weeks to get productive — microservices make that
worse, not better"). Nobody is wrong. Nobody is being unreasonable.
And after three meetings, the team has made exactly zero progress.
**What's actually stuck here — the decision, or something underneath it
nobody has said out loud?**

The staff engineer running the fourth meeting tries something
different: instead of re-arguing the conclusion, she asks each person
to name, out loud, which single factor they'd protect first if they
could only protect one. Priya says deploy velocity. Marcus says
operational risk. Dana says onboarding speed. All three had been
arguing about "what to build," when the real disagreement was "which
of these four things matters most, and by how much" — a question
nobody had actually asked. Once it's on the whiteboard as three
named priorities instead of three restated opinions, the argument
changes shape: from "you're wrong" to "I think risk should count for
more than onboarding speed here, and here's why" — a specific,
resolvable disagreement about one number, not a vague standoff. The
meeting ends in twenty minutes.

## The shape of the problem

Most real engineering decisions above a certain size don't have one
dominant factor — they have several genuinely different ones pulling
in different directions: cost, operational risk, how familiar the
team already is with the approach, how maintainable it'll be in three
years, how fast it gets value in front of users. None of these
collapse into the others. You can't trade "less risk" for "faster
onboarding" using a single formula the way you can trade dollars for
dollars. This is what makes these decisions feel different from a
decision with one uncertain number attached to it (covered in
`expected-value` — how to reason about **one** dimension, like cost,
when its outcome is uncertain). Here the problem isn't uncertainty
about one number; it's that **several numbers matter at once, and
reasonable people weigh them differently.**

When that weighing stays silent — everyone holding their own private
ranking in their head — a group doesn't look like it's disagreeing
about weights. It looks like it's disagreeing about the decision
itself, over and over, with no way to make progress, because nobody
has actually named what they're optimizing for.

## The unlock: make criteria and weights explicit

The fix in the scenario above wasn't a clever new argument for
splitting or not splitting the monolith. It was naming, in writing,
what mattered and roughly how much:

```text
Criteria this decision depends on:
  - deploy velocity      (Priya's top priority)
  - operational risk     (Marcus's top priority)
  - onboarding speed      (Dana's top priority)
  - long-term maintainability (nobody had named this one at all)
```

Writing the list down did two things at once. First, it surfaced a
criterion — long-term maintainability — that nobody had been arguing
for, because nobody had been asked. Second, it turned "we disagree"
into "we disagree about how much weight risk should carry relative to
onboarding speed" — a much narrower, much more debatable claim than
the shapeless argument that came before it. L2 covers the mechanic for
turning that written list into an actual comparable score across
several candidate options; L3 works the whole thing through in real,
runnable code on this exact decision.

## Key terms

- **Multi-criteria decision** — a choice among options where several
  distinct, hard-to-compare factors (criteria) all bear on which
  option is best, and no single number captures all of them.
- **Criterion** — one named dimension a decision depends on (cost,
  risk, maintainability, time-to-value, and so on).
- **Weight** — how much a given criterion should count relative to
  the others, made explicit as a number rather than left as an
  unstated personal priority.
- **Incommensurable** — criteria that can't be converted into each
  other on a common scale (operational risk isn't "worth" some exact
  number of onboarding-weeks) — the reason this differs from a
  single expected-value calculation.
- **Weighted-scoring matrix** — a structured way of combining several
  weighted criteria into one comparable score per option (L2, L3).
- **Sensitivity analysis** — re-checking whether a decision's
  top-scoring option still wins under other reasonable weightings,
  instead of trusting the first weighting tried.
- **Tie-breaker judgment** — when two options score close enough that
  the numeric gap isn't a reliable signal, deciding instead on
  reversibility, who bears the cost of being wrong, or which mistake
  would be more regrettable.
