Four friends walk up to their usual Monday brunch spot. The window sign says
"Closed Mondays." One friend has checked three separate Mondays before and
found it shut every time. Another remembers the one Monday, months ago, when
it was actually open. All three friends below are reasoning from real
evidence, not making anything up.

| What they said                                                            | Good reasoning, bad reasoning, or something in between? |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| "The sign says closed Mondays — it's closed."                             | ?                                                       |
| "We've checked three times, closed every time — basically always closed." | ?                                                       |
| "It was open that one time, so 'always closed' is just wrong."            | ?                                                       |

**Before reading on: are these three arguments all the same kind of reasoning, just with different amounts of evidence — or are they doing fundamentally different things?**

</Scenario>

They're doing fundamentally different things, and telling them apart is the entire point of this unit.

Before the labels arrive, keep four plain-language parts separate:

| Term               | Plain meaning                                                                                                |
| ------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Premise**        | A claim the argument asks you to accept as a starting point                                                  |
| **Conclusion**     | The claim the argument is trying to reach                                                                    |
| **Argument**       | Premises arranged so they are supposed to support a conclusion                                               |
| **Counterexample** | A possible case where the premises hold but the conclusion fails, or a real exception to an overstrong claim |

The sign argument also has a hidden premise that ordinary speech leaves
unstated: "if the bakery follows a current, reliable sign that says
Closed Mondays, then it is closed on Mondays." Without that reliability
premise, "the sign says it" is only evidence about a rule, not the rule
itself.

- **Validity** is a structural property: an argument is valid if its conclusion _must_ be true whenever its premises are true — regardless of whether the premises actually are true in reality. "The sign says closed Mondays. Today is Monday. Therefore it's closed" is valid: if you grant both premises, the conclusion is forced.
- **Soundness** = valid structure + premises that are actually true. A valid argument built on a false premise (maybe the sign is outdated) is still valid — it's just not sound, and the false premise, not the logic, is the problem.
- **Deductive arguments** (like the sign argument) claim their conclusion follows _with certainty_. One counterexample is enough to break them entirely — if the bakery was ever open on a Monday while a fresh, accurate sign said "Closed Mondays," something in the premises was false.
- **Inductive arguments** (like "checked three times, closed every time") don't claim certainty — they claim their conclusion is _probable_, and get stronger with more consistent evidence but never reach deductive certainty. A single counterexample doesn't refute an inductive claim the way it refutes a deductive one — it just means the pattern isn't universal.
- **The third friend's mistake** is treating an inductive claim as if it had made a deductive, universal promise. "Closed every time we've checked" was never a claim that it's closed _every single_ Monday, no exceptions — so one open Monday weakens the pattern's strength, but doesn't logically demolish it the way a counterexample demolishes "always closed, no exceptions" stated as a hard rule.
- **Formal logic** studies argument structure independent of content — the same pattern ("if P then Q; Q; therefore P") is invalid no matter what real-world claims fill in P and Q.
- **Informal logic** studies arguments as they actually occur in language — including exactly this kind of ambiguity, where "basically always closed" and "closed, no exceptions" sound similar but make very different claims.

|                       | Premises true     | Premises false    |
| --------------------- | ----------------- | ----------------- |
| **Structure valid**   | Sound             | Valid but unsound |
| **Structure invalid** | Unsound (invalid) | Unsound (invalid) |

Quick check: **valid** answers "does the structure force it?"
**Sound** answers "does the structure force it, and are the premises
true in reality?" **Convincing** answers a different question: "did the
argument feel persuasive to a human listener?" A claim can be convincing
but invalid, valid but unsound, or sound even if it is stated in a dry,
unpersuasive way.

The practical skill this unit builds: before agreeing or disagreeing with an argument, first ask which of these four boxes it's in — and whether it was ever making a deductive promise in the first place, or just an inductive bet.
