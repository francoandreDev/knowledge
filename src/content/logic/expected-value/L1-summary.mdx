It's the third planning meeting about the database migration, and the room
is still split. One camp wants a slow, staged rollout — migrate 5% of
traffic, watch it, migrate more, watch again — which costs more engineer
time up front but almost never breaks badly. The other camp wants a
big-bang cutover this weekend — cheap and fast if it works, but if it
doesn't, customers see hours of downtime. **Every meeting, the room leans
toward whichever option "feels" safer that week, depending on who's most
anxious or who just came off an on-call shift. Nobody has actually written
down a number. How do you compare two options when neither one has a
single guaranteed outcome — each one is really a whole set of possible
outcomes, each with its own odds?**

Someone finally puts numbers on the whiteboard: staged rollout costs about
$18k if it goes fine (95% of the time) and about $4k if it doesn't (5% of
the time). Big-bang cutover costs about $2k if it goes fine (80% of the
time) and about $60k if it doesn't (20% of the time). **Multiply each
outcome's probability by its cost, and add them up per option, and the
flip-flopping stops** — not because everyone suddenly agrees on gut feel,
but because there's now a number to argue about instead of a mood.

## The tool: multiply probability by payoff, then add

**Expected value (EV)** is a single number that summarizes a decision with
uncertain outcomes: for each possible outcome, multiply how likely it is
by what it costs (or pays), then add those products together. It turns
"this feels risky" into "this costs, on average, $X more than that."

For the migration decision above:

- **Staged rollout EV** = (0.95 × $18,000) + (0.05 × $4,000) = $17,100 +
  $200 = **$17,300**
- **Big-bang cutover EV** = (0.80 × $2,000) + (0.20 × $60,000) = $1,600 +
  $12,000 = **$13,600**

By expected value alone, big-bang cutover looks cheaper — about $3,700
less on average. **But "on average" is doing a lot of work in that
sentence, and L2 spends real time on exactly why that phrase can be
misleading for a decision you only make once.**

## Key terms

- **Expected value (EV)** — the sum, across every possible outcome, of
  that outcome's probability multiplied by its payoff or cost. The
  single-number summary of an uncertain decision.
- **Outcome** — one specific way a decision could play down: "the rollout
  goes fine," "the cutover causes an outage." A decision under
  uncertainty always has more than one possible outcome.
- **Probability** — how likely a given outcome is, expressed as a number
  between 0 and 1 (or a percentage). The probabilities across all of one
  option's outcomes must add up to 1.
- **Payoff / cost** — what an outcome is worth in the units that matter
  for the decision (dollars, hours, engineer-weeks). EV mixes these two
  ideas into one number, so a "payoff" is just a negative "cost" and vice
  versa.
- **Risk tolerance** — how much of a catastrophic, low-probability outcome
  a decision-maker can actually survive, independent of that outcome's
  probability. L2 and L3 both spend real weight on this — it's the
  reason the EV-best option isn't always the right real-world choice.
- **Tail outcome** — a low-probability outcome sitting at the extreme end
  of a distribution (the 20%-chance, $60k outage above). Small
  probability, disproportionate impact.

The whiteboard number didn't end the debate by being "objective" — it
ended the flip-flopping by giving the room a shared calculation to argue
about, instead of a shared mood to be swayed by. Whether $13,600 actually
beats $17,300 as the _right_ choice, once the tail outcome is taken
seriously, is exactly where L2 picks up.
