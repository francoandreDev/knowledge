It's 3am, and an anomaly-detection alert just paged the on-call engineer
and, because of the escalation policy, three other people on the team.
The engineer trusts it — the dashboard has said "95% accurate" ever since
it launched, and 95% sounds like good enough odds to wake people up for.
Everyone spends forty minutes checking dashboards, logs, and recent
deploys. Nothing is wrong. It's a false alarm. **The next morning, someone
finally asks the question nobody asked at 3am: how often does this alert
turn out to be right, given how rare the thing it's watching for actually
is — and is "95% accurate" even the number that answers that?**

They pull the numbers. The event this alert watches for — a genuine,
customer-facing incident — happens in only about 2% of the windows the
detector evaluates. The detector catches 95% of real incidents when they
happen, and it also fires falsely about 5% of the time when nothing is
wrong. Out of 1,000 typical checks: 20 are real incidents, and the
detector catches 19 of them. But the other 980 checks have no real
incident, and 5% of those — 49 checks — still trigger a false alarm.
**Add it up: 68 total alerts, only 19 of them real. When this "95%
accurate" alert fires, it's actually right about 28% of the time — wrong
more often than it's right.** Nobody built a bad detector. Everybody just
read "95% accurate" as "95% trustworthy when it fires," and those are two
different numbers.

## Two traps, one theme: data that looks rigorous but isn't

This unit covers two closely related ways to fool yourself with numbers
that _look_ like evidence:

- **Correlation vs. causation** — two metrics move together, and it's
  tempting to conclude one causes the other. Sometimes it's true. Often
  it's a shared cause (a **confound**), the causal arrow running backward
  (**reverse causation**), or plain **coincidence**.
- **The base rate fallacy** — a signal's own accuracy (how often it's
  right when tested) gets confused with its precision (how often it's
  right when it fires), and those two numbers only match when the thing
  being detected is common. When it's rare, they can be wildly different
  — exactly what happened at 3am above.

Both traps share the same shape: a number that sounds rigorous (a
correlation coefficient, an "accuracy" percentage) substitutes for the
harder question underneath it (is there an actual causal mechanism? does
this account for how rare the event really is?).

## Key terms

- **Correlation** — two quantities tend to move together, statistically.
  On its own, correlation says nothing about _why_.
- **Causation** — one thing actually brings about the other, through a
  real mechanism. This is the stronger, harder-to-establish claim.
- **Confound** — a third factor that independently drives both of the
  correlated quantities, creating the appearance of a direct link between
  them where none exists.
- **Reverse causation** — mistaking which direction the causal arrow
  points: concluding A causes B when it's actually B causing A.
- **Base rate** — how common something actually is in the population
  being checked (2% of checks were real incidents, above) — easy to
  ignore because it isn't part of the alert's own "accuracy" number.
- **True positive rate (sensitivity)** — of the cases where the event is
  real, the fraction the detector correctly flags (95% above).
- **False positive rate** — of the cases where the event is _not_ real,
  the fraction the detector incorrectly flags anyway (5% above).
- **Precision** — of the cases where the detector _fires_, the fraction
  that are actually real (28% above). This, not true positive rate, is
  the number that answers "should I trust this page."

The 3am page wasn't a broken detector or a bad decision by the engineer
who trusted it — it was a base rate nobody put next to the "95% accurate"
number before deciding how much to trust a single alert firing. L2 walks
through exactly how that 95% and that 28% both come from the same real
detector, and why the gap between them gets worse, not better, as the
event being watched for gets rarer.
